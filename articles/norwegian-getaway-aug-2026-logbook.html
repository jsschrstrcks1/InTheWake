Getaway, Day by Day

Norwegian Getaway, Day by Day: Logbook of a Repair Sailing (Aug 24–28, 2026)

Dispatches from aboard the sailing NCL rearranged around propulsion repairs — the overnight in Nassau, the 12-hour island day, and whatever else the week actually delivers. Written from the ship, one day at a time, the good and the bad both.

by Ken Baker | August 24, 2026 — updated daily during the sailing

What this page is: the daily logbook of the August 24–28, 2026 Norwegian Getaway sailing from Miami — the cruise that was rearranged before departure so the ship can overnight in Nassau for propulsion repairs, with Great Stirrup Cay extended to a 12-hour day (8 a.m. to 8 p.m.). I wrote the research before boarding; this page is what actually happens. New entries are added each day of the sailing, newest at the bottom, unpolished on purpose — a logbook, not a brochure. One rule governs the whole week — the same rule that has governed every sailing I've ever written about: the cruise line never learns I write. What you read here is how this ship treats an average guest, because as far as the ship knows, that's exactly what I am.

Day 1 — Monday, August 24: Boarding Smooth, Cabins Not Ready to Be Lived In

Embarkation was smooth. After writing a whole guide to embarkation day from research, I have nothing to correct from the lived version of this one: the terminal did its job and we walked aboard without drama.

The cabins are another story. Our rooms came to us dirty and deteriorated — not tired-around-the-edges dirty, but toenails-on-the-floor dirty, with detritus from the previous guests still in the room. There's no soft way to write that sentence, and I'm not going to try. A cabin is the one space on a ship a family has to itself, and this one had not been made ready for us.

Here is where we've landed, and it's the same standard I'd ask readers to hold us to: we've given the ship the opportunity to fix it. A turnaround day is a hard day for a housekeeping team, and a failure on one cabin — or several — doesn't yet tell you whether you're looking at a bad hour or a bad operation. What happens next is the actual information: how fast, how thoroughly, and with what attitude the ship makes this right. That's tomorrow's entry.

More from the ship tomorrow — including the night we spend plugged in at the pier before the 3 a.m. departure, which the shore power piece promised I'd report on honestly.

Day 1, evening addendum — what the photos show

"Dirty and deteriorated" is a claim, and claims on this site carry evidence. So here is some of what we walked into, photographed as found.

Looking at these, it's worth separating the two problems, because they have different owners and different fixes. The chipped and gouged laminate is deterioration — that's not something a room steward can fix on a turnaround day; it's deferred maintenance, and it tells you something about where this ship sits in her refurbishment cycle. The garment bag, the wristband, the tag scrap, the debris in the carpet — that's dirty, and that one is a cleaning failure with a same-day fix. The ship can't make the furniture new by tonight. It can absolutely make the room clean. That's the part we're watching.

One more Day 1 observation: a rule that bends one direction

Unrelated to the cabin: the hot tubs carry a no-drinks rule, and the enforcement we watched today was strikingly uneven. Guests with alcohol in hand soaked undisturbed. A soda got flagged. Unsweetened tea got flagged. I don't know the policy logic — I can only tell you the pattern as we observed it, and today it ran one direction. A rule that's only enforced against the drinks nobody profits from isn't really a safety rule; it's worth watching whether the rest of the week bears that reading out or corrects it.

Late Day 1: something the research couldn't have told me

There is no breeze on a cruise ship that isn't moving. It's hot in South Florida today, and this sailing doesn't leave the pier until the small hours — so the open decks spend the evening learning what every ship's brochure photo quietly assumes: the wind up there is usually the ship's own speed, made by moving. Tied up alongside, you get whatever the Miami afternoon feels like giving you, which today is not much. File it with the things an itinerary built around staying in port trades away — the overnights in this rearranged week buy repair time and a longer island day, and they pay for it in evenings like this one.

Day 1 dining: O'Sheehan's does the simple thing right

Lunch was O'Sheehan's — say the name aloud with its own Irish lilt and it turns into "oceans," which is a good pun to hang over a pub at sea. We had fish and chips and an Irish Reuben. The fish and chips earned its keep: crunchy outside, moist inside, which is the entire job description of fish and chips and one plenty of kitchens still fail. The Irish Reuben didn't find its balance — too much acid, the kind of sandwich where one element is shouting over the rest. Dinner was a New York strip and a pair of lobster tails, and the verdict came in: the lobster tails were a 10 out of 10 — buttery perfection. The strip was an 8 that would have been a 10 if it hadn't come overcooked. Cheese ravioli in lobster sauce rounded it out — good, and we enjoyed the seafood, though the lobster in that sauce wasn't singing as loudly as the other notes. And then the night's best story: we ordered a Greek salad and were brought bruschetta. The bruschetta was a 9 — excellent. Sometimes the kitchen's mistake outscores your order; we ate it happily and kept the score. Dessert was a warm chocolate lava cake with cookies-and-cream gelato, raspberry coulis, and a mint leaf.

And that's the honest note to end the day's eating on: even with the foibles — an over-acidic Reuben, an overcooked strip, a salad that arrived as bruschetta — it's clear NCL has stepped up their food game. The misses today were execution slips on individual plates; the hits were the kind you don't get by accident.

And one sound from the pier

Off and on all evening, they've been starting the engines and letting them run. I don't know what tonight's runs are — could be checks before the 3 a.m. departure, could be the ordinary rhythm of a ship keeping herself alive at the pier — but it's exactly the kind of detail the shore power piece taught me to listen for, and it goes in the notebook next to the question that article promised this sailing would answer.

Later still: the 3 a.m. departure didn't hold — we're already moving

Update to the update: before 9 p.m., the ship left the pier, hours ahead of the plan. The sailing's Facebook group lit up with the why, and I'll pass it along labeled as exactly what it is — passenger scuttlebutt, some of it secondhand from crew, none of it official. One fellow passenger reported that the test of the shore tie — the system that lets a ship shut her generators down in port — was successful, so there was no need to remain overnight; his bottom line was that the casino could open. Another had read that the propulsion system is running but not at full force, making about 7 knots less than usual. Our waiter at dinner said something in the same family: something technical with the engine that he didn't know the details of.

If the shore-tie report is right, the engine starts we spent the evening listening to may have been that test running — but that's a guess stacked on a rumor, and I'm labeling it as one. Here's what's not rumor: we're underway early, and if the physics note above holds, the open decks just got their breeze back. The shore-power verdict this page owes you deserves better sourcing than a Facebook thread, so it stays open until I can do better than overheard.

Meanwhile, the cabin's list grew

The TV and the door lock are both broken. Maintenance is coming — "apparently," which is the word you reach for after the day we've had with this room. Add it to tomorrow's scorecard: the cleaning, the fixtures, and now the electronics, all waiting on the same question of how this ship responds when something's wrong.

And then the ship answered. "Apparently" turned out to be unfair: someone came to vacuum, the inside of the exterior door handle got repaired, and the TV got fixed — same night, all of it. Credit where due: the answer to how fast started arriving before Day 1 ended. Whether the deeper cleaning matches the vacuum pass is a morning question.

One thread left open, with a prediction attached. When we raised the day's troubles, the answer was that they'd "note it in the report that the customer requested a dinner at the sushi restaurant for his mom." Here's the honest math on that gesture: it's low-hanging fruit. Comp one dinner and they all but guarantee I buy a second seat at the same table — the goodwill costs them almost nothing and probably nets a sale. My mother would appreciate it, and I'd write about it. On the record: I doubt it will happen. The rest of the week gets to score that prediction.

And to be clear about the terms of that test: I did not and will not tell them I write about cruising. That's the integrity of this whole page — if they won't do it for an average person, then they won't be doing it for me either, because an average person is all they know me as. Whatever the ship does next, it does for a nobody. That's the only version of the answer worth printing.

One last Day 1 note, and it's a good one: the air conditioning in this much-discussed cabin is nice and cool — turned all the way down, it was actually too cold. On a hot Miami day with no breeze on deck, being able to overshoot cold is the right problem to have. A pleasant surprise, and it goes on the ledger next to everything else.

Day 1 tech notes: the VPN gauntlet

For the readers who travel with their own network (you know who you are): they managed to block Tailscale on the cheaper internet tier. I traded up to the streaming tier, which explicitly says VPN is enabled — and it delivers: Tailscale works there, and so does plain WireGuard, the protocol Tailscale is built on. And the travel-router trick doesn't seem to work here: a device sitting behind a VPN-enabled travel router still lands on the captive portal. If your plan for this ship was router-does-the-VPN-and-everything-hides-behind-it, plan again. The general lay of the land on ship Wi-Fi is in the internet at sea guide; this paragraph is what Getaway's version of it did to mine on night one.

Day 2 — Tuesday, August 25: Breakfast Done Right, a BOGO, and How This Ship Treats Wheels

Day two started well. We went to breakfast and they got our orders right — which, after last night's Greek salad that arrived as bruschetta, is worth saying out loud. The poached eggs came properly runny in the middle, served over toast. And here's where I got to run a family experiment: growing up, we always had poached eggs with hot milk — poached in the hot milk. They don't poach in milk here, but you can get a cup of hot milk and pour it over the plate, toast and all, and have the whole thing together. It's a northeastern comfort food my mother's family brought to Florida with them, and the improvised shipboard version turned out fantastic. We also ordered the salmon toast — very good, light on the salmon, but with the avocado and everything else on it, fantastic. Beautiful, even.

After breakfast we walked the ship and found out they're running a sale on specialty dining reservations — buy one, get one free. So we're booked into Wasabi tomorrow night for sushi. (Yes, the same restaurant from last night's open thread. We're now going on our own dime either way, which keeps that prediction clean: whatever gesture does or doesn't come, it comes to a family that was already paying.)

How this ship treats wheels

They seem a little more relaxed about wheelchairs on this cruise. One gentleman in his 80s who has trouble walking couldn't get an accessible cabin — and the ship is letting him store his power chair in the hallway. I respect and appreciate that, having taken care of people with disabilities at an earlier point in my life. It's a small accommodation that costs the ship almost nothing and changes that man's week entirely.

The water is a different story. On some Norwegian ships, in our experience, a wheelchair user who wants the pool or hot tub has to give 24 hours' advance notice — and know exactly how many minutes they want to spend in the water. "Come back in an hour or two" is not an option; the lift is not a device they leave up all the time. It appears to be the same way here: I haven't seen a single pool or hot tub lift out and deployed, though I'm told they do exist aboard. That gap — between an accommodation that exists somewhere in a storage room and one a person can actually use on the day they feel like swimming — is the whole subject of our accessibility page, and it's the same gap the Jewel of the Seas lawsuit turns on. I'll keep watching the pool deck this week.

The room gets a staff meeting; the floor keeps its toenails

We came back from breakfast to find a staff meeting about our room being held in our room. I didn't feel heard.

The follow-through since has been better than the meeting. Maintenance came back out for the three doors — two squeaky ones and the third with the bad lock — and there are plans to fix the busted trim from yesterday's photos. The man doing the fixing, Timothy, was great: personable, passionate about his job. Whatever this recovery ends up scoring, Timothy is the part of it that's working.

And yet. Yesterday's vacuum visit notwithstanding, there are still toenails on my floor. The TV got fixed the first night. The lock has now been seen to twice. Three doors made a work order. And the thing that started this whole thread — the floor a family walks on barefoot — still hasn't been cleaned. That's the sentence I'd most like to delete from this page, and I can't yet. Let's see what happens.

One piece of irony to file with the sushi prediction: what I actually asked for — I book one dinner, they comp my mother's — is more or less their own deal of the day. It's the same buy-one-get-one that already has us booked into Wasabi tomorrow night. The gesture I predicted they wouldn't make is one they're literally running as a promotion. The prediction stands; the bar for beating it just got lower.

Day 2 lunch: room service, scored

We ordered lunch from room service: flank steak, a Cobb salad, and a BLT. The BLT tasted excellent — but order two, it's small. The Cobb salad was a 6 out of 10: the chicken was dry, and they didn't put the balsamic vinaigrette on it that I asked for — though the blue cheese they chose was fantastic. The flank steak was an 8 out of 10: overcooked but still tender and moist, and the chimichurri was fantastic too.

Two days in, the kitchen pattern is holding steady: the flavors keep landing — chimichurri, that blue cheese, yesterday's buttery lobster — while the misses stay in the same two columns, doneness (Monday's New York strip came overcooked, and now Tuesday's flank did too — two overcooked steaks in two days) and order accuracy (a missing vinaigrette after yesterday's wandering bruschetta). Good kitchen, loose execution. It's a fixable kind of flaw, which is exactly why it's worth writing down.

After lunch we went up to Spice H2O and grabbed a table. The hot tubs and pool were full, but we found a shady spot — which, on a hot afternoon with the ship underway, is all the real estate you actually need.

Day 2 dinner: the chowder finds the family

We ordered the bruschetta again — on purpose this time — and it was once more fantastic. The spinach artichoke lacked seasoning, salt especially. The parmesan-crusted pork chop had the opposite problem: salty, and diminished by the canned tomatoes on top. Same kitchen, same night, one dish underseasoned and the next oversalted — that's the loose-execution column again, twice in a single course.

The bluefish was a 10 out of 10 — flavorful, well balanced, wonderful.

But the standout for me was the New England clam chowder, and that's not a bar it cleared easily. My grandfather's family lived in New England clear back to the early days — minutemen, for instance — and my grandmother, a Floridian, learned their chowder and made it her own. This one gives hers a run for its money. Smoky, unctuous, flavorful. It would have benefited from a little heat — chop a few jalapeños into the pot — but otherwise it's one of the best clam chowders I've had in decades. Easily tied with my clam chowder experience in Alaska, and maybe better.

The promised dessert report: what I ordered was a chocolate something with a scoop of ice cream. What arrived was two scoops of ice cream. Haha. More of the same — that's the third order this week that came back as a different dish than the one requested, and by now we're taking it as the ship's version of a scratch-off ticket. The bruschetta proved you can win these. Two scoops of ice cream is a push.

Day 2, evening: the '90s, twice

After dinner we went to a '90s pop-culture show — full of the bright Nickelodeon, "Saved by the Bell" colors, slightly campy on purpose, running through Friends, Blockbuster, Bop It, boy bands, the whole shelf of the decade. All in all, a good show. The comedy show we caught afterward was mediocre — but at least it didn't reuse the same six jokes every comedian on a ship tells, which is its own kind of achievement.

Day 2, late: the toenail ledger closes — mostly

Our room steward came back and did a much better job with the vacuum today, and I no longer see toenails on my floor. Is it perfect? No. But it is acceptable now.

Which leaves the question this whole thread has been circling, and I'll leave it exactly the way I asked it: what is the acceptable number of toenails? On a cruise? Some questions answer themselves — and it took from Monday's boarding to Tuesday to get the count near where it should have started.

They did offer us a load of laundry free. So that's something, at least. The honest ledger on the cabin now reads: fixed slowly, fixed eventually, acknowledged modestly. And for the record — laundry wasn't the wager. The sushi prediction stays open for tomorrow night.

Day 3 — Wednesday, August 26: The Kitchen Splits the Difference

Breakfast: Belgian waffles, steak and eggs — two eggs over easy — a fruit bowl, and a side of bacon. The waffles were very good, and so was the blueberry syrup. The eggs came with nice runny yolks. The fruit bowl was fresh, ripe, and nicely presented. And the steak — after two days of overcooked — was cooked appropriately. Credit where due: the doneness column just posted its first win of the sailing.

Two misses, both from familiar columns: the bacon never came, which makes it the fourth order this sailing to arrive different from what was asked. And the very good waffles were also, unfortunately, cold — both things true at once, which is its own kind of kitchen review. The pattern through three days holds: this galley can cook; it's the last stretch between the pan and the plate where things go sideways.

Nassau, on foot

We wandered Nassau: saw the Queen's Staircase and walked down it from the top, did some shopping in port, and had conch fritters. Then back to the ship for a nap — which is its own honest review of a port day that asked nothing more of us than that.

The staircase earns its reputation. The interpretive plaque at the site tells the story better than I could paraphrase, so here is what it says: named for Queen Victoria, who became queen in 1837, the staircase was chiselled from solid limestone circa the 1830s by free men of African descent — "The 66 Steps," cut through the remains of a stone quarry, standing 31 meters tall as the public access between Fort Fincastle and the city. Paving over the years has reduced the count to 64. The plaque calls it "a symbol of resilience, highlighting the enduring spirit of the Bahamian people," and standing at the bottom of those walls, that doesn't read like brochure language.

Wasabi: a clean sweep

Dinner at Wasabi was outstanding. Start to finish, everything was a 10 — and that includes Jewel, our waitress, who took great care of us. After a sailing of eights-with-an-asterisk and very-good-but-cold, a meal where every plate and every touch lands deserves to be said plainly: no asterisks tonight.

As for Monday's prediction — tonight was the buy-one-get-one we booked ourselves, so the question of whether the ship's promised gesture for my mother ever materializes on its own stays open and unscored. Credit tonight where it's due: on the plate, and in Jewel's hands.

Day 4 — Thursday, August 27: A Good Island Day, and a Last Night That Went Wrong

Largely good, right up until it wasn't. That's the honest shape of the last full day, and I'm going to write it in that order rather than let the ending swallow the rest.

The pier question, answered

When I wrote the Great Stirrup Cay piece before sailing, I couldn't resolve the pier. The sources conflicted, I said so, and I promised this sailing would settle it. It did: the pier is open. It's a temporary pier while the permanent one is still being built, and it is a long walk.

That last detail is the one worth carrying. If you're standing in your cabin wondering whether to bring the chair or leave it, bring it — it's a long walk from the ship to anywhere you actually want to be. And on the honest question of whether the island works for a wheelchair user: everything isn't fully accessible, but it's better than other islands. That's not a glowing review and it isn't meant to be. Among tender-and-sand private islands, "better than the others" is a real thing to know.

What things cost out there — from the signs

Two boards worth photographing, because private-island pricing is hard to find before you're standing in front of it. The zipline board, as posted: Osprey (three lines over the beach) $99 adults, $79 kids; Seahawk (three lines over the ocean plus climbing) $99 adults, $79 kids; Island Combo, both tours, $139 adults, $119 kids. And the line that isn't in any brochure, quoted from the sign itself: "For all tours and rental on Great Stirrup Cay, there will be a 10% Bahamian sales tax added to the advertised prices." The posted number is not the number, and by that wording it applies to rentals too — chairs, floats, whatever else you were budgeting at face value.

The safety board next to it matters more than pricing for some readers, because it decides whether the attraction is available to you at all. As posted: minimum age 8, with under-18s accompanied by an adult; weight between 50 and 250 pounds (23–113 kg); minimum height four feet (122 cm); pants or shorts and a shirt; closed-toe shoes or sandals with a heel strap — it names Crocs and Keens — and no slip-ons, flip flops, or bare feet. Riders must be in good physical condition and, in the sign's own words, "able to climb a 20-foot ladder unassisted." It's also not recommended for anyone with balance problems, epilepsy, or heart, back, neck or shoulder conditions.

That ladder requirement is the sentence I'd want if I were planning around a bad knee or a heart condition, and it appears nowhere in the marketing. It's also the difference between an attraction being on the island and an attraction being available to you — a distinction this week kept teaching in different rooms.

Three schedules for one sailing

They changed the schedule again. The itinerary-change letter NCL sent before we sailed put the island call at 8 a.m. to 8 p.m.; we left port earlier than that — around 6, though I'm going from memory and not a printed time, so treat that figure as unconfirmed until I can pin it. Count it up: the original itinerary, the rearranged one in the letter, and then what actually happened. Three versions of one week. Nothing about that ruined the day, and it's worth knowing if you're the kind of planner who builds a shore day around the posted hours.

The lift question stays open — and here's why

Four days aboard, and I never saw a pool or hot tub lift deployed. Not once.

I did talk with a man in a wheelchair who was satisfied with the accommodations he'd been given — and I want to be careful with that, because it would be easy to file his answer as the answer. It isn't. He doesn't swim and won't use hot tubs, so the question of whether a lift arrives when a wheelchair user actually wants the water is one he never had to ask. His satisfaction is real and it is about something else. So the honest report is: the accommodations he needed worked, and the thing our accessibility page keeps pressing on — whether the lift is a device you can use on the day you feel like swimming, or a device that exists in a storage room — is still unanswered by this sailing. I'd rather leave that open than close it with someone else's unrelated yes.

The prediction, scored

On Monday I wrote that the ship had promised to "note it in the report that the customer requested a dinner at the sushi restaurant for his mom," that comping it would cost them almost nothing and probably sell a second seat, and that I doubted it would happen. It didn't happen. They never followed through.

I take no pleasure in being right about that. The gesture I described was the ship's own running promotion — the same buy-one-get-one that had us at Wasabi on our own dime the night before. Declining to do for a guest what your marketing is already advertising is a choice, and it's the choice they made.

Moderno: two dollars and seventy-five cents

And then the last night went wrong in a way I'm still turning over.

One of us had a $16 pending credit and a $2.75 pending charge sitting on a cash account. That was enough for the system to "close" the ship card — and once it was closed, we weren't free to leave the restaurant until somebody figured it out. It took an hour to eat and another hour of asking them to bring us the bill.

Meanwhile the clock the ship itself sets was running: luggage had to be out. So I left my mother at the table with the cards and the cash, and went to get our bags into the hallway before the deadline — and I made it with a quarter-hour to spare. As I walked out, the waiter was yelling after me about resolving a "financing issue." Two dollars and seventy-five cents.

While I was gone, the employees were rude with an elderly woman in my own family to the point of tears. There's no version of that sentence I can soften, and I'm not going to try.

We climbed the chain until we reached Hristina — pronounced like Christina, without the C. She apologized profusely, said she would write the problem up and re-train the employee, and asked what she could do for us. We told her nothing. It was the last day. We get off tomorrow. We might not be back; let's see. I want that on the record precisely because it's the part that can't be bought: when the ship finally offered to make something right, we declined, because by then the only honest thing left to want was for it not to have happened.

She handled it the way you'd want it handled, and I'll name her for that. I'm not naming the employees. They were wrong, and they were also standing inside a system that turned a rounding error into a hostage situation on the busiest night of the week — and a page that Google indexes forever is not the right instrument for a line-level worker's bad hour. The training failure belongs to the ship.

Then the billing went wrong a third time this week: two charges for the same photo.

Here's the part I'd want a reader to take away, because it isn't about rudeness. NCL pushes you to prepay, and when an account goes sideways the beverage package is the first privilege stripped — the thing you already paid for is the thing they take first. A cash account doesn't behave like a credit card on file; pending amounts don't clear the same way, and a card that locks on the final evening does maximum damage, because that's the night the bill closes and the bags go out. The mechanism deserves its own piece, and it's getting one.

And the actual cost, the one that has nothing to do with money: we missed the Fleetwood Mac tribute concert. My mother loves that band. She'd been looking forward to it, and instead she spent the hour being spoken to that way. The hour they took was the hour of the show.

That's the last full day: an island that answered a question we'd published as unresolved, signage worth photographing, an accessibility gap still honestly open, a promise not kept, and a family walking back to a cabin at the end of it. Largely good. Then not.

Day 5 — Friday, August 28: Off the Ship, and a Pavilion Over the Water

Disembarkation was quick, easy, and orderly. We dragged our feet on purpose and ended up among the last people off, which is the move I'd recommend to anyone who isn't chasing a flight: let the crowd go, let the terminal empty, walk off into a building that isn't a scrum. Breakfast beforehand was a little chaotic — but no more than you'd expect on the last morning in the main dining room, when everyone in the room is eating with one eye on a luggage tag. I'm not counting that one against them.

So the week ends the way it began: the parts of this ship that move people did their job well. Boarding was smooth on Monday, getting off was smooth on Friday, and almost everything that went wrong in between happened in a cabin, at a table, or on a folio.

Then we did the thing that turned the day around. We picked up hot pressed Cubans and took them out to the Everglades for a picnic. There's a pavilion out over the water there with rocking chairs, and it usually catches a good breeze — which, after a Monday night on a ship that wasn't moving, is a joke the week set up for us without meaning to. We sat and rocked and ate and thoroughly enjoyed it.

That's a fair place to leave it for now. A cruise is five days of a company's decisions and one afternoon of your own, and the afternoon of our own was the best part. Whether the week as a whole was worth it — and what I'd tell somebody looking at this ship — is a verdict I want to write with my feet on the ground and a little distance, not in the parking lot. It'll go here when it's ready.

The Verdict: Great Strides, Still Weighed Down

I said I'd write this with some distance rather than in the parking lot. Here it is.

Norwegian is making great strides against where they were before Covid. The shows are better and so is the food. I don't hand that over lightly — this page spent the week scoring their kitchen plate by plate, and the scores are still sitting up there: a clam chowder that gave my grandmother's a run for its money, a bluefish at ten out of ten, a Wasabi dinner that didn't miss once, a '90s show that was genuinely good. Somebody inside that company decided those things were worth money and effort, and it shows.

What weighs them down is everything around the plate: a lack of attention to detail, and customer-service policies that put Norwegian first.

The detail problem ran the whole week. Four orders came back as something other than what was asked for. A cabin arrived unfit to be lived in and took until Tuesday to reach acceptable. The billing was wrong three separate times, including two charges for one photograph. None of that is catastrophic on its own. All of it together is a company that isn't checking its own work.

The policy problem is the serious one, because it isn't an oversight — it's a design. A pending charge of two dollars and seventy-five cents was enough to close a ship card and hold a family in a restaurant on the last night. Norwegian pushes you to prepay, and when an account goes sideways the beverage package is the first thing stripped — the thing you already bought is the thing they take first. And when a manager offered to make something right, what got promised was a note in a report that never became anything. Those aren't accidents. Somebody chose each one, and each choice protects Norwegian rather than the person who paid.

Notice who isn't on that list: the crew. Timothy, who fixed what he could and clearly cared about doing it well. Jewel, who ran a flawless table. Hristina, who took the complaint seriously and apologized like she meant it. The people on this ship were not the problem this week — they were repeatedly the thing that rescued it. That distinction matters, because a company can rewrite a policy in an afternoon, and it takes years to build a crew worth defending.

So, the bottom line. I haven't decided whether I'll sail Norwegian again. My mother has. She won't.

That's the whole review, really. A cruise line spent real money getting the shows and the food right, and then lost a passenger over two dollars and seventy-five cents and the way someone spoke to her when her son had stepped away. The kitchen isn't what they need to fix.
