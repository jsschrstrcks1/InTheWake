The Single Supplement: Why One Person in a Cabin Often Pays for Two

The advertised fare was never for you. What the lines actually say about solo pricing, and the four levers that shrink it.

by Ken Baker | September 5, 2026

Answer first: the price you see advertised is per person, and it assumes two people are sharing the cabin. Travel alone in an ordinary cabin and most lines charge you both halves. Royal Caribbean writes the number down: "A single guest who wishes to have a stateroom to themselves will be expected to pay 200% of the category rate." The way out is not haggling. It is booking a ship that has cabins built and priced for one person — Norwegian's solo staterooms, where "Double occupancy rates don’t apply" — or accepting the supplement and collecting what compensation the line offers for it.

What the advertised price actually means

Every fare on every cruise line's home page is a per-person, double-occupancy number. The cabin is being sold to two people and the price tag shows one person's half. Nothing about that is hidden — it is stated in the small print on the page — but it is small print, and the effect is that a solo traveller reads a number that was never for them.

Royal Caribbean states the mechanism directly: "All rates posted on this website are based on double occupancy. A single guest who wishes to have a stateroom to themselves will be expected to pay 200% of the category rate, unless otherwise noted in the pricing."

Two hundred percent. Not a fee added to the fare — the whole fare twice. That is the single supplement, and it is why a $899 balcony week is a $1,798 balcony week for one person.

Why it exists

This part is my explanation, not a quotation, so weigh it as reasoning rather than as a line's stated policy. A cabin costs the ship roughly the same to sell, clean, service and sail whether one person or two is in it. The berth is the scarce thing, not the head. If a line let solo travellers pay half, every cabin sold to one person would earn half the revenue of the identical cabin next door while costing nearly the same to run — and on a ship where every cabin is inventory, that difference is the whole margin.

What the supplement does not reflect is the onboard spend. A solo traveller drinks one drink package, eats one specialty dinner, buys one excursion. Some of what the second passenger would have spent is genuinely lost to the line, and that is part of the arithmetic too.

Whether 200% is the right answer to that problem is a fair argument. It is simply the answer most lines have chosen.

The way out: cabins built for one

The real fix is not a discount on a two-person cabin. It is a smaller cabin designed for one person and priced as one person.

Norwegian has gone furthest here, and says so plainly: "Solo Staterooms are priced for one" and "Double occupancy rates don’t apply. Solo Staterooms are always priced for one." It lists four solo categories — Solo Balcony, Solo Oceanview, Solo Inside and Solo Studio — with the first three described as available fleetwide and Solo Studios on fewer ships.

One correction worth having before you book, because it is repeated wrongly nearly everywhere: the Studio Lounge is not included with every solo cabin. Norwegian's own page carries the qualification — "Studio Lounge is only available for Solo Studio accommodations." If the private lounge with the complimentary coffee and espresso is part of why you are booking, you need a Solo Studio specifically, not a Solo Inside or Solo Balcony. Book the wrong one and the key card simply will not open the door.

The four levers that make the supplement smaller

  1. Book a ship that has solo cabins. This is the only lever that removes the supplement rather than reducing it. It also constrains your itinerary to the ships that have them, which for some destinations means no.
  2. Take the loyalty compensation if you are paying it anyway. Royal Caribbean awards an extra point per night to a solo guest paying double occupancy — a seven-night balcony sailing earns fourteen points instead of seven. That is not money back, but if you are within sight of a tier it is worth counting.
  3. Watch for reduced or waived supplements as a promotion. Lines run these, especially on sailings that are not filling. They come and go and are not a policy you can plan around, which is why there is no list of them here.
  4. Consider the cheaper cabin rather than the cheaper line. Two hundred percent of an inside cabin is often less than one hundred percent of a solo balcony, and an inside cabin on a ship you like beats a balcony on a ship you do not.

What I cannot tell you

I cannot tell you what your sailing will cost, and I am not going to guess. Solo pricing moves with demand more sharply than double-occupancy pricing does, because solo inventory is small and fills unevenly. The 200% figure is Royal Caribbean's stated policy, not a market rate, and the phrase "unless otherwise noted in the pricing" is doing real work in that sentence — a promotional sailing can carry a lower supplement without any announcement.

I also cannot tell you which ships have how many solo cabins today. Norwegian has expanded the category repeatedly and other lines have added and quietly removed them. The only reliable answer is the deck plan for the specific ship and sailing date you are considering.

One thing worth saying plainly

Solo travellers are not a rounding error and they are not, mostly, people who failed to find a companion. They are widowed, they are divorced, they are between things, they are married to someone who gets seasick, they are going because they wanted to go. The supplement is a pricing decision, not a judgment — but it lands like one, and it is worth naming that it does.

The good news is that the industry has moved. Purpose-built solo cabins barely existed fifteen years ago and now run to the high hundreds across one line's fleet alone. If you want to sail alone, you can — and increasingly you can do it without paying for a person who is not there.
