When Your Cruise Cabin Isn't Clean: What to Do

Every hotel occasionally hands someone a room that was not ready. What happens next is the part that tells you something.

by Ken Baker | September 5, 2026

Short answer: photograph it before you touch anything, report it the same day through your room steward and guest services, ask for a specific fix rather than an apology, and give the ship a genuine chance to make it right before you decide what kind of problem you are dealing with. Most cabins get fixed. How fast, and how completely, is the real information.

Photograph it first

Before you unpack, before you move anything, take pictures. Wide shot of the room, then close-ups of each specific thing. Get the floor, the bathroom, inside the closet, under the desk, the mattress edge if it is bedding.

This is not about building a case for a fight. It is about the fact that memory is a bad witness and rooms change. Once your suitcase is open and your things are on the surfaces, nobody can tell what was there when you arrived — including you. Photographs taken in the first five minutes cost nothing and settle every later question about what you actually walked into.

We did this on a recent sailing and it changed the whole conversation. When the room was dirty in a way that is hard to describe politely, the photographs meant we never had to argue about whether it was as bad as we said.

Report it the same day, through two channels

Your room steward first. They are the person who can actually fix most of it, and they are usually the person who wants to. Be specific and be kind — turnaround day is genuinely brutal for housekeeping, and the person in front of you may not be the person who missed it.

Guest services second, the same day. This is the part people skip, and it matters. Telling the steward gets the room cleaned. Telling guest services gets it recorded. If the problem recurs, or if you later need someone senior to care, a report logged on day one is the difference between a documented pattern and your word.

Ask for both: the fix, and confirmation that it has been logged.

Ask for a specific fix, not an apology

The single most useful change you can make to how you complain is to name the outcome you want. "The room is dirty" invites sympathy. "There is debris on the floor and in the closet, and I need the carpet vacuumed properly and the closet cleared before tonight" invites action, and it gives you something to check later.

Separate two different categories while you are at it, because they have different owners and different timelines:

  • Dirty — debris, dust, someone else's belongings, uncleaned surfaces. This is a cleaning failure and it has a same-day fix. It is reasonable to expect it resolved before you sleep in the room.
  • Deteriorated — chipped veneer, gouged trim, worn fittings, broken drawers. This is deferred maintenance. No room steward can fix it during your cruise, and asking them to is asking the wrong person. It is still worth documenting, because it tells you something real about where that ship sits in its refurbishment cycle — but it is not a same-day ask.

Holding those apart keeps your complaint credible. A guest who demands the furniture be replaced by dinner gets filed under unreasonable, and everything else they said gets filed there too.

What a reasonable response looks like

Here is the standard we hold, and we think it is a fair one: give them the opportunity to fix it, then judge them by the response rather than the failure.

Any hotel operation, floating or otherwise, will occasionally hand someone a room that was not ready. A single bad cabin tells you one team had a bad turnaround. What happens next is what tells you whether you are dealing with a bad hour or a bad operation.

A good response looks like: someone comes the same day, the specific things you named get addressed, and somebody with authority acknowledges it without making you feel unreasonable for raising it. A poor response looks like: agreement that it is unacceptable, followed by nothing, or a fix so partial that you have to ask again.

On our most recent sailing the ship landed somewhere in between, and the honest verdict was that it took from boarding on Monday until Tuesday to reach a state we would describe as acceptable rather than good. The electronics and fixtures were fixed the first night; the actual cleaning took a second pass and a second conversation. That whole sequence is documented day by day, including the photographs.

When and how to escalate

Escalate when a specific, reasonable, same-day request has been made and has not been met — not because you are angry, but because the first channel has demonstrably not worked. Ask for the housekeeping supervisor or the guest services manager. The chain of command and what actually moves it is covered in its own guide.

Keep asking for the same specific thing. The strength of your position comes from consistency and documentation, not volume.

About compensation

You may be offered something — a complimentary service, a dinner, a laundry credit. Two honest observations.

First, do not lead with it. A complaint that opens with what you want in return reads as a transaction, and it makes staff defensive rather than helpful. Ask for the room to be fixed. Compensation, if it comes, comes after.

Second, decide in advance what would actually settle it for you, because "what can we do for you?" is a question people answer badly under pressure. It is entirely acceptable to say nothing, or to say you would rather they simply finish the job.

Knowing when to stop

At some point a room becomes acceptable without becoming perfect, and you have to decide whether to keep pushing. That is a judgment call, not a rule, and the right answer depends on how much of your week you want to spend on it.

Our own line: once the health-and-decency problems are gone, we stop. Cosmetic wear on an older ship is a fact about the ship, and no amount of escalation converts deferred maintenance into a refurbished cabin. Record it, write it down honestly, factor it into whether you sail that ship again — and then go enjoy the cruise you paid for.

The short version

  • Photograph everything before you unpack.
  • Tell the room steward, then tell guest services the same day, and ask that it be logged.
  • Name the specific fix you want and a time by which you want it.
  • Separate dirty (same-day) from deteriorated (not their fault, not fixable this week).
  • Judge the ship by its response, not by the failure.
  • Escalate on facts, not volume — and stop when it is acceptable.
