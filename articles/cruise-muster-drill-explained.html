Embarkation Day, Step by Step

The Cruise Muster Drill: What It Is Now and What You Actually Have to Do

It is shorter than it used to be, and it is still the most important thing you do on embarkation day.

by Ken Baker | September 5, 2026

Short answer: it is mandatory, it happens before the ship sails, and on most modern ships it is now two parts — watch the safety briefing on your phone or cabin TV, then go to your assembly station in person so a crew member can confirm you did. It takes a fraction of the time the old version did, and it is still the most important thing you do on embarkation day.

What a muster station is

Royal Caribbean's definition is the clearest short one: a muster station is "a designated emergency assembly point on a cruise ship where passengers must report during safety drills or real emergencies."

That is the whole concept. In an emergency, thousands of people need to be somewhere specific and countable, quickly. The drill exists so that you already know where your somewhere is, and so the crew has practised getting you there.

What changed, and why it feels different now

If your last cruise was years ago, you remember the old form: everyone summoned by alarm at a set hour, crowded shoulder to shoulder on a deck or in a lounge, some ships with life jackets, a long briefing delivered to a restless crowd.

Most major lines have replaced that with a staggered, partly self-service version — Royal Caribbean calls its implementation the Guest Safety Briefing, commonly known as Muster 2.0. The line describes it as two parts: guests "access everything they need right from their smartphone or the stateroom TV," and then "visit their assembly station, where the crew can verify they've completed the process."

The safety content is the same content. What changed is the delivery: you take in the briefing individually, at your own pace, and the physical step becomes a short check-in rather than a mass assembly. Which is genuinely better — a crowd standing in the sun is not an environment in which anybody absorbs safety information.

It is not optional, and it is not a formality

Royal Caribbean describes it as a "mandatory safety practice" and states plainly that "there are no shortcuts when it comes to safety." Behind the line's own policy sits international maritime regulation requiring a muster for embarking passengers around departure. I am stating that in general terms on purpose: I could not fetch the regulatory text itself, so this page cites no convention, chapter or article number, and you should not quote one from here. What is solid either way is the practical part — this is not a company preference, and crew have no discretion to wave you through.

Practically, that means the ship tracks completion. Expect to be chased if you have not done it — announcements, a call to the cabin, staff sent to find you. Every year some passengers treat this as an inconvenience to be dodged, and it is worth saying the unglamorous thing: the drill is the one piece of the cruise that exists entirely for the scenario where things go badly wrong, and the people asking you to complete it are the people who would be trying to get you off the ship.

How to get it done quickly

  • Do the app or TV portion as soon as you board — before lunch, before the pool, before the cabin is ready. It is short, and doing it first removes the only thing that can chase you all afternoon.
  • Find your assembly station assignment. Lines commonly print or display it on your cruise card and in the app; check both. If you cannot find it, ask any crew member — they are asked this constantly and will point you.
  • Go in person and get scanned or ticked off. The verification is the part that counts. Completing the video alone does not finish the process.
  • Everyone in the cabin, individually. Children too. One person cannot complete it on behalf of the party.
  • Walk the route once. While you are there, notice how you got there from your cabin — which stairs, which side, which deck. That thirty seconds of attention is the actual point of the exercise.

If mobility is a factor

Tell the crew at your assembly station. If you use a wheelchair or scooter, cannot manage stairs, or would need assistance evacuating, that information is far more useful to them recorded on day one than discovered in an emergency. This is also worth raising with the line's accessibility team before you sail — the same principle as everything else in our accessibility guidance: the accommodation that gets arranged in advance is the one that exists when you need it.

Details worth knowing

Life jackets. Practice varies — some ships have you handle one, some do not, some show you where they are stored. Whatever your ship does, find out where yours is kept in the cabin and how the straps work. That takes a moment and is useful knowledge.

Boarding late. If you board close to sailing, you will still be required to complete it, usually promptly and with less flexibility. Another argument for not cutting embarkation fine — our embarkation guide covers the rest of that timing.

Ships vary. Not every ship or line uses the same implementation, and some smaller vessels still run a traditional assembly. Your daily programme and cabin TV will tell you which version you are on; that is the authoritative source for your sailing, not this page.

The short version

  • Mandatory, before sailing, tracked — you will be chased if you skip it.
  • Usually two parts: briefing on app or cabin TV, then in-person verification at your assembly station.
  • Do the app portion the moment you board.
  • Every guest individually, children included.
  • Note the route from your cabin — that is the part that could matter.
  • Flag mobility needs to the crew at the station and to the line beforehand.
