# Abidjan, Côte d'Ivoire — Cruise Port Research Dossier

**Prepared:** 30 July 2026
**Purpose:** Source base for an authoritative ~5,000-word port guide for cruise passengers.
**Occasion:** Holland America Line *Volendam*, 2028 Grand World Voyage (129 days, departs Fort Lauderdale 4 January 2028).
**Method note:** Primary sources preferred throughout — Port Autonome d'Abidjan (PAA), WHO International Health Regulations Annex 1, US Department of State Consular Affairs data API, UK FCDO, Ivorian national census (RGPH 2021), ICCO, BCEAO. Distances computed from OpenStreetMap/Nominatim coordinates and OSRM road routing rather than repeated from travel blogs. Every figure carries its source. Where sources conflict, the conflict is reported rather than resolved silently.

---

## 0. Executive summary for the guide writer

Abidjan is a **working cargo port with no cruise terminal of any kind**. The PAA's own "key figures" page enumerates nine terminals — container ×2, fruit, fishing, bagging/conventional, ro-ro, petroleum, ship repair, grain, mineral — and **no passenger or cruise facility** ([PAA, Les chiffres clés](https://www.portabidjan.ci/fr/le-port-dabidjan/chiffres)). Cruise calls are berthed opportunistically at whichever commercial quay is free, most recently the grain terminal. The port is a **closed customs enclosure** in which public access to the quays and aprons is prohibited by regulation and a pass from the Port Commander is required to move around inside it. A passenger therefore cannot simply walk off the ship and out of the gate on foot; getting ashore means organised transport.

Everything else — safety, health, visas — flows from a single practical reality: this is a Level 2 / partially-Level-4 country where the risk that actually applies to a day visitor at the port is **ordinary urban crime and traffic**, not terrorism, and where the terrorism advisories that dominate search results describe a northern border region **550–700 km away** from where the ship ties up.

## 1. The berth and getting ashore

### 1.1 Where cruise ships actually tie up

There is **no cruise terminal and no dedicated passenger berth**. PAA's inventory of its own installations lists nine terminals, none of them for passengers ([PAA, Les chiffres clés](https://www.portabidjan.ci/fr/le-port-dabidjan/chiffres); [PAA, Installations et activités](https://www.portabidjan.ci/fr/le-port-dabidjan/installations-et-activites)).

The most recent and most specific statement from the port authority itself: when *Norwegian Sky* (260 m) and *Riviera* (232 m) berthed **simultaneously on Thursday 28 November 2024** — a first for Abidjan — they did so at *"le site du nouveau terminal céréalier du Port d'Abidjan"*, the new grain terminal, which PAA describes as *"Bâti sur une superficie de 9,5 hectares avec 700 mètres de linéaires de quai et un tirant d'eau de 13,5 mètres"* and which has been *"opérationnel depuis mai 2023"* ([PAA news, 28 Nov 2024](https://www.portabidjan.ci/fr/actualites/le-port-dabidjan-accueille-deux-navires-de-croisieres-le-ms-norwegian-sky-et-le-ms)). PAA adds that the grain terminal *"accueille fréquemment des navires de croisière"* — it frequently receives cruise ships. **This is the single best-sourced statement of where a cruise ship berths in Abidjan.**

**Geographic location — resolved.** OpenStreetMap addresses the *Terminal Céréalier Port Autonome Abidjan* at **5.30319 N, 4.02666 W**, addressed to *"Quai Nord (Q1–Q5), Zone 2b, Treichville, Abidjan"*. The berth is therefore on the **north side of the port basin, in the commune of Treichville**, roughly 400 m from PAA's own headquarters on Avenue Francis Wodié. Treichville's Wikipedia entry independently confirms that *"the Autonomous Port of Abidjan operates here"* ([Treichville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treichville)).

**Caveat on the quay identity — a genuine three-way conflict in PAA's own publications.** The grain terminal is described with three mutually inconsistent sets of figures by the same authority:

| Source | Quay length | Max draft |
|---|---|---|
| [PAA news release, 28 Nov 2024](https://www.portabidjan.ci/fr/actualites/le-port-dabidjan-accueille-deux-navires-de-croisieres-le-ms-norwegian-sky-et-le-ms) | 700 m | 13.5 m |
| [PAA, La Capitainerie](https://www.portabidjan.ci/fr/offre-de-service/la-capitainerie) — terminals summary table | 665 m | 12.5 m |
| [PAA, Terminal céréalier](https://www.portabidjan.ci/fr/offre-de-service/terminal-cerealier) — "quais QC1, QC2, QC3" | — | 12.5 m |
| [PAA, Installations et activités](https://www.portabidjan.ci/fr/le-port-dabidjan/installations-et-activites) | 350 m | 8.7 m |

The **Capitainerie (harbourmaster) table is the operationally authoritative one** — it is the document masters and agents work from — and it gives the grain terminal **665 m of quay at 12.5 m admissible draft**. The 350 m / 8.7 m figure on the "Installations" page appears to describe the *old* grain berth and is stale relative to the May 2023 facility. The 700 m / 13.5 m in the press release is promotional rounding. **For the guide, use 665 m / 12.5 m and note that all figures below are comfortably in excess of what *Volendam* needs.**

Note also that the Capitainerie's berth table separately lists **Quai Nord (Q1–Q5), 775 m, 9.45 m draft**, with the annotation *"Q1 et Q3 pour le débarquement de céréales"* — grain discharge also happens at the older Quai Nord berths. Since OSM addresses the grain terminal to "Quai Nord (Q1–Q5)", it is possible that the "new grain terminal" is an extension or redevelopment within the Quai Nord complex rather than a wholly separate facility. **Marked UNVERIFIED:** the exact berth designation (QC1–QC3 vs Q1–Q5) a given cruise ship is assigned. The practical answer for a passenger is unchanged: north side of the port basin, Treichville, a working grain quay.

### 1.3 Does *Volendam* fit? — Yes, with wide margin

*Volendam* specifications: **61,214 GT, 237.6 m LOA, 32.3 m beam, 8.3 m draught, 1,837 passengers / 647 crew, Fincantieri Marghera, entered service 1999, Rotterdam (R) class** ([MS Volendam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MS_Volendam)).

*(The brief's figures of ~237 m LOA and ~8.1 m draft are essentially right; Wikipedia's 237.6 m / 8.3 m is the more precise pair. Design draught varies slightly with loading condition.)*

Against the port's published limits:

- **Vridi Canal entry/exit limit (PAA Capitainerie):** *"Longueurs hors tout et tirants d'eau autorisés à l'entrée dans le canal de Vridi : Entrée : 50,8 pieds (15,5 m) / Sortie : 50,8 pieds (15,5 m)"* — a **15.5 m draft limit, with no length limit published**. *Volendam*'s 8.3 m is 7.2 m inside it. ([PAA, La Capitainerie](https://www.portabidjan.ci/fr/offre-de-service/la-capitainerie))
- **Air draft:** *"Canal de VRIDI : aucune obstruction aérienne"* — no overhead obstruction. There is **no lock**; the Vridi Canal is an open cut, not a locked waterway. (Same source. The brief's phrasing "channel/lock constraint" — there is no lock.)
- **Grain terminal draft:** 12.5 m admissible (Capitainerie table). Fits.
- **Beam:** 32.3 m. The Capitainerie's 32.20 m "PANAMAX" beam ceiling applies specifically to the **petroleum jetties** (PETROCI, PUMA ENERGY), not to the general cargo quays; container quays routinely take post-Panamax beams. *Riviera* at 32 m and *Norwegian Sky* at 32.5 m both berthed. No issue.

PAA also states that the port has received a **400-metre vessel** — *"une première rendue possible grâce à la vision du Chef de l'État"* ([PAA](https://www.portabidjan.ci/fr/actualites/le-port-dabidjan-accueille-un-navire-de-400-metres-une-premiere-rendue-possible-grace-la)). Length is emphatically not a constraint.

### 1.4 The Vridi Canal — dimensions, history, constraints

**The canal is the port's only sea entrance.** Abidjan sits on the Ébrié Lagoon, which is separated from the Gulf of Guinea by a sand barrier; the Vridi Canal is the artificial cut through that barrier.

*History (PAA's own account, [Historique](https://www.portabidjan.ci/fr/autorite-portuaire/historique)):*
- *"Les premières tentatives de percement du littoral qui eurent lieu à l'Ouest de Port-Bouët (près de Petit-Bassam) en **1906 et 1907** se soldèrent par des échecs"* — the first two attempts, west of Port-Bouët, failed because littoral drift closed the openings within weeks.
- Engineer **Roger Pelnard-Considère** proposed the successful oblique-cut design in **1932–33**; it was model-tested at the **Delft hydraulics laboratory** in the Netherlands and definitively approved in **December 1935**.
- Construction began **1938** and continued through the Second World War.
- **23 July 1950**: *"l'on fit sauter le dernier « bouchon » du canal de Vridi"* — the last plug was blasted and the lagoon met the ocean.
- **5 February 1951**: the port formally opened, inaugurated by **François Mitterrand**, then French Minister for Overseas Territories. First vessel through was the *Marie Dominique*; the first ocean-going ship was the *Volta*.

*(Wikipedia's [Vridi Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vridi_Canal) stub says only "constructed in 1950" and takes its name from the village of Vridi. PAA's account is far richer and is the primary source.)*

*Current dimensions after the 2019 widening/deepening:*
- Inaugurated **February 2019** ([PAA project page](https://www.portabidjan.ci/fr/le-port-dabidjan/projet-de-developpement/elargissement-approfondissement-du-canal-de-vridi-inaugure); [Ministère des Transports](https://www.transports.gouv.ci/actualites/port-autonome-dabidjan-inauguration-du-canal-de-vridi-elargi-et-approfondi)).
- **Before:** PAA states the project context as *"Impossibilité d'accueillir des navires de plus de 260 m de longueur et de plus de 11,50 m de tirant d'eau"* — vessels over 260 m LOA or 11.5 m draft could not be accommodated.
- **After:** PAA's stated expected result: *"Possibilité d'accueillir des navires sans limitation de longueur avec 16 m de tirant d'eau"* — **no length limitation, 16 m draft**.
- PAA's Installations page gives the canal as **300 m wide and 18 m deep**; the Capitainerie's operational limit is **15.5 m draft**. These are compatible — dredged depth vs. admissible draft with underkeel allowance. Some press reports give the **entrance pass** widened to **350 m from 200 m** ([Financial Afrik, 22 Feb 2019](https://www.financialafrik.com/2019/02/22/cote-divoire-avec-le-redimensionnement-du-canal-de-vridi-le-port-dabidjan-retrouve-sa-competitivite/)). **The 300 m figure is PAA's own and should be preferred for the canal; the 350 m applies to the entrance pass specifically.**
- PAA/press state passage is now possible *"quelles que soient les conditions de courant ou de marée"* — **regardless of current or tide**. There is therefore **no tidal window constraint** for a ship of *Volendam*'s draft. (Tidal range at Abidjan is small — this is a microtidal coast — but the operative point is that PAA no longer treats tide as a gating factor.)

*Navigation rules that shape the arrival ([PAA, La Capitainerie](https://www.portabidjan.ci/fr/offre-de-service/la-capitainerie)):*
- Port position given as **5°19′N 4°01′W**.
- Entry request 72 h before arrival at Vridi roadstead and at least 6 h in advance, via the ship's agent and the online booking system `conference.paa.ci`.
- Vessel reports to **Vridi signal station ("vigie Abidjan")** on **VHF 12 or 16** at the pilot boarding limit. *"Aucun navire ne peut entrer, sortir ou faire mouvement dans le port s'il n'y a été autorisé par le Commandant du port."*
- **Pilotage compulsory 24/7** for vessels over 150 NRT. **Towage compulsory** over 150 NRT, provided by IRES. **Mooring (lamanage) compulsory** over 150 NRT, provided by SLA.
- Anchoring in the entrance pass is **formally prohibited**, as is anchoring within 75 m of the canal banks.
- Any berthed vessel must be able to vacate its berth within **3 hours** if ordered by the Port Commander — a working-port rule that occasionally matters to cruise schedules.

**Passenger-facing translation:** the approach is a scenic one. The ship comes in off the open Gulf of Guinea, takes a pilot, threads the ~2.5 km Vridi cut with Port-Bouët and the airport to port and the Vridi industrial zone to starboard, then opens into the lagoon and turns into the port basin. The berth is roughly **5.4 km** from the canal mouth (straight-line, Nominatim coordinates), so allow perhaps 45–60 minutes from pilot boarding to all-fast.

### 1.5 Is it a secure/industrial facility passengers cannot walk out of? — Yes

This is the most important practical fact in the entire guide, and it is directly sourced. PAA's Capitainerie page, under *Accès au port*:

> *"Le libre accès sur les voies, les quais et les terre-pleins à l'intérieur de l'enceinte douanière du Port d'Abidjan est interdit au public. Pour pouvoir pénétrer et circuler dans l'enceinte portuaire toute personne ou tout engin doit être muni d'un titre d'accès délivré par le Commandant du port."*

— Free access to the roads, quays and aprons inside the **customs enclosure** of the Port of Abidjan is **prohibited to the public**. To enter and move about within the port precinct, **any person or vehicle must hold an access pass issued by the Port Commander**. ([PAA, La Capitainerie](https://www.portabidjan.ci/fr/offre-de-service/la-capitainerie))

The port also maintains a dedicated **Département Sûreté** (ISPS security), a **Gendarmerie du Port**, and a **Commissariat du Port**, with a security VHF watch on channel 13 (same source). This is an ISPS-regulated industrial estate handling containers, grain, fuel and vehicles — not a promenade.

**Consequences for a passenger:**
- You **cannot walk out of the gate**. There is no pedestrian route out of a working grain terminal surrounded by container yards, tank farms and truck traffic, and the regulation forbids it in any case.
- Getting ashore means **a vehicle**: the ship's shuttle if one is run, a ship excursion coach, or a pre-arranged car/tour that has been cleared into the port to collect you.
- **Taxis at the gate** are not something to plan on. Taxis cannot enter the customs enclosure without a pass; the practical arrangement at ports of this type is that ship-organised transport takes passengers to a drop point outside the gate, from which taxis are available. **UNVERIFIED:** whether HAL will run a complimentary port shuttle at Abidjan in 2028, and to where. HAL has not published 2028 port logistics. Treat "there will be a shuttle" as an assumption, not a fact.
- The ship's gangway area will be a **cargo apron**: uneven concrete, no terminal building, no seating, no shops, no reliable Wi-Fi, no taxi rank. Expect a tent or a marquee at best. PAA's news releases describe cultural welcomes for cruise calls but never a terminal building.

## 2. Safety — honestly and precisely

### 2.1 United States — Department of State

**Level 2: Exercise Increased Caution.** Reissued **18 February 2026** (the Consular Affairs data API timestamps it `2026-02-17T19:00:00-05:00`, i.e. 18 Feb UTC), with the note *"There was no change to the advisory level or risk indicators. Advisory summary was updated."*

Full text retrieved from the [Consular Affairs travel advisories API](https://cadataapi.state.gov/api/TravelAdvisories); canonical page [travel.state.gov Côte d'Ivoire](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/traveladvisories/traveladvisories/cote-d-ivoire-travel-advisory.html) (Cloudflare-blocked to automated fetch, hence the API). Mirrored by [US Embassy Abidjan](https://ci.usembassy.gov/travel-advisory-cote-divoire-february-18-2026/).

Headline: *"Exercise increased caution in Côte d'Ivoire due to **crime, terrorism, unrest, health**, and **piracy** in nearby waters. Some areas have increased risk."*

**Level 4 — Do Not Travel: "the northern border region"**, due to crime and terrorism. The advisory names **Savanes and Zanzan Districts, including Comoé National Park**, as where attacks have occurred, and identifies **JNIM** (al-Qa'ida-linked, Sahel-based) as the main threat, with **AQIM** conducting small-scale attacks. It notes the Ivorian state's response: the **Northern Operational Zone (ZON)**, the **Counterterrorism Operational Intelligence Center (CROAT)**, and the **International Counterterrorism Academy (AILCT)**. Critically, it states: *"There have been **no recent known incidents of violent extremism in the region**, although risks remain along the northern border and in the northeast corner."*

**Movement restrictions on US government employees** (a useful proxy for real risk assessment): they *"are not allowed to drive outside major cities at night and require two-car convoys for official travel outside of coastal and major north/south highways."* Note what this does **not** restrict: daytime movement within Abidjan, and travel on the coastal highway — which is exactly the Abidjan–Grand-Bassam road. The State Department also notes it *"has a limited ability to provide routine or emergency services to U.S. citizens **outside of Abidjan**"* — Abidjan is the one place where consular help is nearby.

**Crime section, verbatim:** *"Violent crime, including carjacking, robbery, and home invasion, is common in some areas. Local police often lack resources to respond to serious crimes. Stay aware of your surroundings in urban areas and crowded markets. Police roadblocks may be present throughout the country, and though not common recently, unofficial roadblocks may be encountered in some locations."*

**Terrorism target list:** spots frequented by foreigners, night clubs, schools, hotels, restaurants, places of worship, international diplomatic missions, local security forces and civilians.

**Piracy:** *"Ships at sea near Côte d'Ivoire are vulnerable to major threats from piracy, armed robbery, and kidnapping for ransom. U.S. citizens on vessels in the Gulf of Guinea off West Africa should stay alert."* This is a real regional issue for merchant shipping. It is **not** a meaningful risk to a 61,000 GT cruise ship with high freeboard, high speed and a Best Management Practices posture, and Gulf of Guinea piracy incidents have fallen sharply since their 2020 peak. Mention it, contextualise it, do not alarm.

### 2.2 United Kingdom — FCDO

**Still current at 30 July 2026; last updated 29 May 2026.** ([FCDO Côte d'Ivoire travel advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/cote-d-ivoire))

FCDO does not use numbered levels; it uses zones:

- **Advises against ALL travel to:**
  - **within 40 km of the borders with Burkina Faso and Mali**
  - **Northern Zanzan and Savanes provinces**
  - **Comoé National Park**
  Reason: *"increased risk of terrorist attacks and kidnaps by terrorists based in the Sahel region."*
- **Advises against all but essential travel to: within 20 km of the border with Liberia**, *"owing to the risk of serious violence by local militias"* / clashes between local militias and security forces.
  ([FCDO Warnings and insurance](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/cote-d-ivoire/warnings-and-insurance); [Regional risks](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/cote-d-ivoire/regional-risks))

**Abidjan is in none of these zones.** FCDO has no adverse advice against travel to Abidjan, Grand-Bassam, or anywhere on the coast east of the Liberian border area.

The 29 May 2026 update was specifically *"Addition of information about heightened health screening measures for those entering Côte d'Ivoire from Ebola-affected regions"* — see §3.6.

### 2.3 Are the northern regions relevant to a cruise passenger? — No

**They are not, and the guide should say so plainly.** The Level 4 / all-travel-against zones are 550–700 km from the berth by road. To reach the nearest of them (Comoé National Park's southern edge) from Abidjan is a full day's drive each way. No cruise excursion goes anywhere near them; the entire published Abidjan excursion menu operates within a 40 km radius of the port.

The honest framing: **the country carries a Level 2 for reasons that are real, but the specific Level 4 geography has nothing to do with a port day.** What a passenger should actually calibrate against is the crime and traffic advice, not the Sahel advice. Search results, news coverage and AI summaries all foreground the jihadist threat because it is the dramatic part; for this trip it is the least relevant part.

### 2.4 Crime patterns that actually affect a day visitor

FCDO's crime advice for Abidjan, verbatim ([Safety and security](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/cote-d-ivoire/safety-and-security)):

> *"There is a risk of violent crime in Abidjan, including armed break-ins to private residences and armed hold-ups in the street. **Though not common, they do occur.**"*

FCDO's street-crime countermeasures, in its own order:
- use taxi apps or pre-arranged private taxis
- **avoid using public transport after dark**
- make sure your accommodation has security measures
- keep valuables out of sight; do not wear expensive-looking jewellery or watches
- **do not walk after dark**
- **take transport to use ATMs or to exchange money at the bank** (i.e. do not walk to an ATM)
- keep your bank card in sight when paying, to prevent card fraud

**Reading this for a port day:**
- **Walking in Plateau in daylight is normal.** Neither FCDO nor State prohibits or discourages daytime walking in Abidjan; the prohibitions are specifically *after dark*. Plateau on a weekday is a functioning CBD with office workers, banks, ministries and police presence. Phone snatching and pickpocketing in crowds are the realistic risks — hold the phone, do not navigate one-handed on the street edge, keep the camera on a wrist strap.
- **The ship will be back aboard before dark on a standard call**, which removes the single largest risk factor in both advisories.
- **Markets are the pickpocket environment.** State specifically flags *"crowded markets."* Treichville's Grand Marché is exactly that. Go with a guide, carry little, and keep hands on bags.
- **Taxi risk is about the vehicle and the fare, not violence.** FCDO: *"Vehicles used as taxis tend to be old and there may be issues with the standard of maintenance. Ride-hailing apps are also available, and vehicles operating on these apps tend to be better maintained. **Although no taxi can be guaranteed safe, we recommend using ride-hailing apps where possible.**"* The specific scam pattern for a foreigner is the unmetered-fare negotiation (see §6.4).
- **Roadblocks and checkpoints** exist in and around Abidjan. FCDO: slow down, stay calm, comply with routine document requests; police should issue paperwork with any fine. Beware unofficial roadblocks by *coupeurs de route* — but FCDO places these *"mostly on secondary or quiet roads"*, and State says they are *"not common recently."* Not a port-day issue on the Abidjan–Bassam highway.
- **Vehicle crime by armed robbers** is specifically located by FCDO on *"the main roads in the **west** of the country"* — not the Abidjan–Grand-Bassam corridor.
- **You must carry your passport.** FCDO: *"You must carry your passport. Carry the original as the police will not always accept copies."* This collides with normal cruise practice (leave the passport in the safe, carry the ship card). See §4.5 — this needs a decision from the ship.

### 2.5 Political stability, elections, protest history

**This matters, and 2025 was not a quiet year.**

- **Presidential election, 25 October 2025.** **Alassane Ouattara won a fourth term with 89.77%** of the vote; Jean-Louis Billon second at ~3%, Simone Gbagbo third at ~2.4% ([AllAfrica, 28 Oct 2025](https://allafrica.com/stories/202510280077.html)).
- **Turnout was about 35%**, amid an opposition boycott ([ISS Africa](https://issafrica.org/iss-today/cote-d-ivoire-s-presidential-election-entrenches-democratic-fragility)).
- **Major opposition candidates were barred from the ballot**; hundreds were arrested when protesters challenged the exclusions ([CIVICUS Lens](https://lens.civicus.org/democracy-confined-cote-divoires-elections/)).
- The constitutional question: the constitution sets a **two-term limit**, but the government-aligned Constitutional Council ruled that the **2016 constitutional changes created a new republic and reset the count** — the same argument used in 2020. ([ISS Africa](https://issafrica.org/iss-today/cote-d-ivoire-s-presidential-election-entrenches-democratic-fragility); [International Crisis Group](https://www.crisisgroup.org/africa/west-africa/cote-divoire/318-electoral-puzzle-handling-cote-divoires-high-risk-poll))
- **A two-month ban on public protests was decreed on 17 October 2025.** Incidents were reported in **Abidjan, Bonoua, Dabou, Daloa, Divo, Gagnoa, Grand-Moutcho and Yamoussoukro** ([ISS Africa](https://issafrica.org/iss-today/cote-d-ivoire-s-presidential-election-entrenches-democratic-fragility)).
- In **August 2025**, tens of thousands protested against what organisers called a slide into authoritarianism ([Peoples Dispatch, 13 Aug 2025](https://peoplesdispatch.org/2025/08/13/tens-of-thousands-protest-in-ivory-coast-against-the-slide-into-dictatorship/)).

FCDO's standing language: *"Political demonstrations sometimes take place in Abidjan and regional towns, particularly around elections. The authorities may use tear gas to disrupt unauthorised protests. Crowds can gather quickly, and roadblocks are common. It is possible that, in the event of significant unrest, **internet access might be restricted**."* Also: *"Be alert near military barracks and military installations."*

**For a 2028 call:** the next presidential election is due in **2030**, and legislative elections were held in **December 2025**. **April 2028 should fall in a politically quiet stretch of the cycle** — no scheduled national vote. That is a real, checkable reason for cautious optimism, and it should be stated with the reasoning shown rather than as reassurance. Municipal/regional elections are held on a separate cycle; **UNVERIFIED:** whether any local poll falls in 2028.

**Historical context matters for tone.** Abidjan is a city that saw open urban warfare within living memory — the Battle of Abidjan in March–April 2011 (see §8.6). Visitors sometimes expect visible scars. Fifteen-plus years on, the city is rebuilt, the skyline is growing (Tour F is under construction and would be sub-Saharan Africa's tallest), and the economy has averaged **7.1% annual growth 2012–2023, Africa's second-fastest** ([Ivory Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivory_Coast)). Do not write a war-damage guide.

## 3. Health entry requirements

### 3.1 Yellow fever — REQUIRED, from everyone, from everywhere

**This is the single most important pre-cruise action item, and it is not negotiable.**

The authoritative source is **WHO's International Health Regulations Annex 1**, *"Countries with risk of yellow fever transmission and countries requiring proof of vaccination against yellow fever"*, dated **18 November 2022, revised 3 January 2023** ([WHO PDF](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/travel-and-health/countries-with-risk-of-yellow-fever-transmission.pdf)). The row for **Côte d'Ivoire** reads:

| Country | At risk of YF transmission (WHO) | Requires proof from travellers arriving from at-risk countries | Requires proof from travellers arriving from **ANY country** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Côte d'Ivoire** | **Yes** | – | **Yes (≥ 9 months)** |

**Read that carefully.** Côte d'Ivoire sits in the "Any country" column. The requirement is **not** conditional on where you have been. **Every traveller aged 9 months and over must present a valid International Certificate of Vaccination or Prophylaxis (ICVP, the "yellow card") against yellow fever, regardless of country of origin or itinerary.** Only a handful of countries impose the requirement this broadly; Côte d'Ivoire is one of them.

Corroborated independently by:
- **UK FCDO:** *"To enter Côte d'Ivoire, you must have a certificate to prove you've had a yellow fever vaccination."* ([Entry requirements](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/cote-d-ivoire/entry-requirements))
- **UK NaTHNaC / TravelHealthPro:** *"Under International Health Regulations, a yellow fever vaccination certificate is required from all travellers over 9 months of age."* ([TravelHealthPro Côte d'Ivoire](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/country/112/cote-divoire-ivory-coast))
- **CDC Yellow Book:** yellow fever vaccination required for all travellers ≥9 months; recommended for the whole country. ([CDC Yellow Book 2024, Côte d'Ivoire](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/yellowbook/2024/preparing/yellow-fever-vaccine-malaria-prevention-by-country/ivory-coast))

**Certificate validity:** Since **11 July 2016**, under the WHA67.13 amendment to IHR Annex 7, a yellow fever certificate is valid for the **life of the person vaccinated**. WHO's Annex 1 footnote is explicit: *"valid certificates of vaccination presented by arriving travellers cannot be rejected on the grounds that more than 10 years have passed since the date on which the vaccination became effective, as stated on the certificate. Boosters or revaccination cannot be required."* **A certificate from 1998 is still valid in 2028.**

**Timing:** the certificate becomes valid **10 days after vaccination** and the vaccine should be given at least 10 days before arrival.

**What this means for a cruise passenger going ashore for a day.** Do not assume a day visit is exempt. In practice:
- **The ship will require it.** Cruise lines sailing West Africa routinely make a yellow fever certificate a **condition of boarding for the whole voyage** when any port on the itinerary requires it, because the line — not the passenger — is liable to the port state for landing a non-compliant passenger. On a 129-day world cruise touching Ghana, Togo/Benin, Senegal and Côte d'Ivoire, expect HAL to demand the yellow card at check-in in Fort Lauderdale in January 2028, not at Abidjan in April.
- **Passengers who cannot be vaccinated** (age, immunosuppression, egg allergy, thymus disorder, pregnancy) need a **medical waiver letter on the ICVP itself**, signed by a physician at a designated yellow fever vaccination centre. WHO/IHR provides for this, but acceptance is at the discretion of the port health authority and **cannot be guaranteed**. Anyone in this category must resolve it with HAL's medical/guest services **before** booking, not before sailing.
- **Practical action:** the vaccine is only available at **registered/designated centres** (TravelHealthPro), so book early. It is a live attenuated vaccine with real, if rare, serious adverse events — TravelHealthPro notes it *"carries specific risks requiring professional assessment."* Over-60s taking it for the first time face a higher risk of YEL-AVD/YEL-AND and need a genuine risk conversation, not a rubber stamp. On a world-cruise demographic this is not a hypothetical.

### 3.2 Malaria

**High risk throughout the country, all year, including Abidjan.** ([TravelHealthPro](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/country/112/cote-divoire-ivory-coast); [CDC Yellow Book](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/yellowbook/2024/preparing/yellow-fever-vaccine-malaria-prevention-by-country/ivory-coast))

- **Chloroquine resistance is present.** Chloroquine is not an option.
- **Recommended chemoprophylaxis** (UK NaTHNaC): **atovaquone/proguanil** (start 1–2 days before, continue 7 days after); **doxycycline** 100 mg (start 1–2 days before, continue 4 weeks after; not for under-12s); **mefloquine** 250 mg (start 2–3 weeks before, continue 4 weeks after). CDC additionally lists **tafenoquine**.
- **The judgement call for a cruise passenger.** A one-day daylight port call in an urban core is a materially lower exposure than a week upcountry — mosquito density in central Plateau is low and *Anopheles* bite mainly dusk to dawn, and the ship will be back at sea by evening. But West African world-cruise itineraries typically string together **several** West African calls over 10–14 days, and the cumulative exposure is what matters. Some passengers reasonably take a full course across the African leg; some rely on bite avoidance alone. **This is a decision for a travel-medicine clinician who can see the full itinerary, not a decision for a guidebook.** What the guide should say: the risk is real, it is chloroquine-resistant falciparum, atovaquone/proguanil is the usual choice for short exposures because of its 7-day tail, and **bite avoidance (DEET 50%, long sleeves at dusk) is not optional whatever you decide about tablets**.
- **Fever after the voyage is a medical emergency until malaria is excluded.** *P. falciparum* can present up to a year later. Tell any doctor you were in West Africa.

### 3.3 Other vaccinations

**For most travellers** (TravelHealthPro): **hepatitis A, tetanus** (booster if >10 years), **typhoid**, **yellow fever** (mandatory, above).
**For some travellers**, depending on activity and duration: hepatitis B, rabies, meningococcal ACWY, polio booster, cholera, measles/MMR, BCG, plus advisories on chikungunya, dengue and Zika.
**Practical minimum for a one-day port call:** yellow fever (mandatory), hepatitis A and typhoid (food/water route, genuinely relevant), tetanus up to date. Rabies pre-exposure is generally not warranted for a single urban day but is a reasonable choice on a 129-day voyage touching many countries.

### 3.4 Water and food

TravelHealthPro: take care with food and water hygiene; **drink boiled, filtered or bottled water**. Tap water in Abidjan is not reliably safe for a visitor.
Practical rules: bottled water with an intact seal, no ice from unknown sources, no raw salads or peeled-by-someone-else fruit, hot food served hot. **Avoid freshwater contact entirely** — schistosomiasis is present, and the Ébrié Lagoon is not for swimming for several reasons.
Note also FCDO's **ocean warning**: *"Ocean currents are very strong along the coast. Rip tides cause many swimming accidents and drownings each year. There is no lifeboat or rescue service."* This applies squarely to Grand-Bassam's beach, which looks inviting and is not. **Do not swim at Grand-Bassam.**

### 3.5 Medical care ashore

FCDO: *"Medical facilities in Abidjan are of a reasonable standard but can be expensive. Emergency facilities are limited to a few major hospitals. Outside of Abidjan medical facilities are rudimentary."* Serious treatment may require medical evacuation. Emergency number **185** for an ambulance.
State Department: *"Hospitals and doctors may require payment up front prior to service or admission... There are often shortages of medicine, medical supplies, and basic resources... **Pharmacies are not well regulated. Locally available medications may be unsafe.**"*
**Guide line:** the ship's medical centre is the right first stop for anything short of a true emergency; do not buy medication ashore; carry your own supply of anything you need.

### 3.6 Ebola screening — current as of 29 May 2026

FCDO, added 29 May 2026: *"As a public health measure, Côte d'Ivoire has introduced screening for travellers arriving from countries affected by the Ebola outbreak, including the **Democratic Republic of the Congo and Uganda**. You should expect to provide health information, undergo health screening, and may be monitored for up to 21 days after arrival. The World Health Organisation (WHO) has declared this a Public Health Emergency of International Concern."* ([FCDO Entry requirements](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/cote-d-ivoire/entry-requirements))

**Relevance to the 2028 call:** the *Volendam* itinerary does not touch DRC or Uganda. The measure is origin-specific and would not apply. But **any active screening regime slows pratique and gangway clearance**, and outbreak status in 2028 is unknowable today. Flag it as a live variable, not a current obstacle. Expect a health declaration form and possibly thermal screening at the gangway regardless.

## 4. Visa and immigration

### 4.1 The formal position: a visa is required, and the e-visa does not help you

**FCDO, unambiguous:** *"**You must have a visa to enter Côte d'Ivoire.**"* And on the e-visa: *"Apply and pay for an e-visa online at least 10 working days before you travel to allow time for your application to be processed. Once it is approved, **you will be able to collect your visa on arrival at Abidjan airport**."* Non-ECOWAS nationals *"must hold a valid visa or have had your application for an e-visa approved before you travel."* ([FCDO Entry requirements](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/cote-d-ivoire/entry-requirements))

**The e-visa system** is operated by **SNEDAI**, mandated by the Ivorian ministries, at **https://snedai.com/e-visa** ([SNEDAI](https://snedai.com/e-visa/)). Key features:
- Cost around **€73** including bank fees, for a **3-month multiple-entry** visa; approval typically in **48 business hours**.
- Pre-enrolment requires uploading passport identity pages, **a return flight booking**, and a letter of invitation or accommodation certificate.
- **Final enrolment (biometric fingerprint capture) happens on arrival.**
- **Critical limitation:** *"L'e-Visa Côte d'Ivoire « SNEDAI » ne permet l'entrée dans le pays que par l'Aéroport International Félix Houphouët-Boigny d'Abidjan"* — the e-visa is **valid only for entry at Abidjan airport**. It is **not valid at the seaport**. ([SNEDAI](https://snedai.com/e-visa/); corroborated by multiple visa agencies including [Action Visas](https://www.action-visas.com/visa/Cote-d'Ivoire-e-Visa))

So the e-visa route is structurally unavailable to a cruise passenger: it demands a flight booking you do not have, and it lands you at an airport you are not arriving at.

**Passport validity:** at least **6 months** beyond arrival date (FCDO).

### 4.2 What actually happens on a cruise call — and the honest limits of what is documented

**No official Ivorian source that I could locate publishes a cruise-passenger visa exemption.** This is the single largest documentation gap in this dossier, and the guide must be honest about it rather than assert an exemption.

What can be established:
- Cruise ships have called repeatedly — at least six documented calls 2023–2026 — with passengers going ashore on organised excursions to Grand-Bassam, Banco, Bingerville and Plateau. PAA's own news releases describe passengers touring the country. Whatever the mechanism, **it works in practice**.
- The mechanism at ports of this type is a **collective/group shore pass** arranged by the ship's agent with the *Police de l'Air et des Frontières* / immigration: the ship presents a passenger manifest, immigration grants temporary shore leave against the manifest and the ship's undertaking to re-embark everyone, and no individual visa is issued. This is standard for cruise calls in much of West Africa. **It is an arrangement between the line's agent and the state, not a right the passenger holds.**
- Some national guidance is passport-specific. Australian sources report no visa required for Australian passport holders arriving **by cruise ship in transit** where accommodation is aboard and the call is under 12–24 hours — but this is passport-specific and not an Ivorian government statement. Forum evidence shows **cruise lines and immigration sources giving conflicting information** ([Tripadvisor Ivory Coast forum thread](https://www.tripadvisor.com/ShowTopic-g294192-i9911-k14505829-Arriving_Ivory_Coast_by_cruise_ship-Ivory_Coast.html) — inaccessible to automated fetch; content per search index).

**MARKED UNVERIFIED — do not state as fact:** that cruise passengers are formally exempt from the Ivorian visa requirement. What can be stated as fact: the e-visa is airport-only; a visa is formally required for entry; and every documented cruise call has landed passengers successfully under arrangements made by the ship.

### 4.3 Independent passengers vs. ship excursions — the visa dimension

This is where the distinction bites hardest, and it compounds the port-access problem from §1.5.

- **On a ship excursion:** you are covered by the ship's collective arrangement, you are manifested, you are transported through the port gate on a cleared coach, and the ship is contractually obliged to get you back.
- **Independently:** you may find that the shore pass is issued only for organised movement, or that you are permitted ashore but cannot get through the gate without pre-arranged transport, or that you are permitted freely. **You will not know until the day**, and the answer can differ between calls and between immigration officers.

**The guide's operative advice:** anyone intending to go independently at Abidjan must ask HAL — in writing, well before sailing, and again at the port talk aboard — three specific questions: (1) does the ship require a visa for this port; (2) is independent shore leave permitted; (3) is there transport through the port gate for independent passengers. Vague reassurance is not an answer to any of them.

### 4.4 Cash import limit

FCDO: *"You can take up to **500,000 West African CFA francs** in cash into Côte d'Ivoire. You must **declare any foreign currency**."* (~€762 at the fixed parity.) Not a practical constraint for a day ashore, but the foreign-currency declaration obligation is worth a line.

### 4.5 The passport-carrying conflict

FCDO: *"**You must carry your passport.** Carry the original as the police will not always accept copies."* State Department's standing advice, by contrast, is *"Carry a copy of your passport and visa (if applicable). Leave originals in your hotel safe."*

These genuinely conflict. Aboard ship, the passports are usually held by the purser on a world cruise, and the normal shore document is the ship's card plus a photocopy. **Resolve this with the ship, not with the guidebook.** The realistic outcome is that HAL issues or retains documents per the local agent's instruction; passengers should follow that instruction and carry a colour photocopy plus the ship card as a minimum.

## 5. What to actually see on a port day

### 5.1 St Paul's Cathedral, Plateau — the one unmissable building

**Cathédrale Saint-Paul d'Abidjan**, 5.33204 N, 4.02025 W, Avenue Jean-Paul II, Le Plateau. **4.0 km / ~5 min free-flow from the berth** (realistically 15–30 min).

- **Architect: Aldo Spirito**, Italian. ([St. Paul's Cathedral, Abidjan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Paul's_Cathedral,_Abidjan))
- **Foundation stone laid 11 May 1980 by Pope John Paul II** on his first pastoral visit to Côte d'Ivoire.
- **Dedicated on completion, 10 August 1985**, during John Paul II's second visit — the same trip on which he laid the foundation stone of the Yamoussoukro basilica.
- **The design:** a great concrete cross, anchored to the main body of the cathedral by **seven cables**, creating the illusion that the whole structure is being drawn toward the **Ébrié Lagoon**. The commonly repeated reading is that the mass of the building represents a figure — variously described as St Paul, or a supplicant — with the cross as the head and the swept roof as a cloak. It is one of the most spectacular pieces of late-modernist religious architecture anywhere in Africa.
- **Exterior:** coloured ceramic panels narrating the life of Christ.
- **Interior:** **six stained-glass windows covering 370 m²**, combining biblical scenes with Ivorian subjects — including, famously, a depiction of the arrival of missionaries in Côte d'Ivoire. The glass is the reason to go inside.
- **Cost:** approximately **US$12 million**; described as reputedly the second-largest church on the African continent as of 2002.
- **Can you go in? Yes.** It is an active parish and metropolitan cathedral of the Archdiocese of Abidjan, and it is the first stop on every documented cruise excursion here ([PAA, Jan 2026](https://portabidjan.ci/en/news/cruise-ship-seven-seas-voyager-calls-again-abidjan-port)). **UNVERIFIED:** published visiting hours and any admission or photography fee. In practice access is generally free outside service times; a small donation is customary; dress modestly (FCDO: *"Dress modestly when visiting religious places or buildings"*). Sunday morning means Mass — beautiful, crowded, and not a sightseeing slot.

### 5.2 Plateau — the skyline, La Pyramide, the Cité Administrative

**Plateau is Abidjan's CBD**, on the north shore of the lagoon, connected to Treichville by the Houphouët-Boigny and Charles de Gaulle bridges. It holds the parliament, the Constitutional Council and the Supreme Court — **Abidjan retains the machinery of government even though Yamoussoukro is the political capital** ([Abidjan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abidjan)).

- **The skyline** is the reason Abidjan gets called "the Manhattan of the lagoon" (and, more tiresomely, "the Paris of West Africa"). It is genuinely striking from the water — the best view is from the ship on the way in, and from the lagoon.
- **La Pyramide** — the emblematic building and a cautionary tale. Designed by Italian architect **Rinaldo Olivieri**, built **1968–1973**, in Plateau. Olivieri's stated intent was *"to capture the activity of an African market in an urban setting"* — housing in the pyramidal body above, retail at ground level. It **deteriorated badly from the 1990s** amid political instability and poor space efficiency, and became hazardous in the 2000s. In **2011 the government announced a public-private renovation at an estimated 18 billion CFA francs** to turn it into a tourist attraction — **but no work has commenced**. ([La Pyramide (building)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Pyramide_(building))) **As of the most recent sourcing available, it is a landmark to photograph from outside, not a place to visit.** Do not write it up as an attraction.
- **Tour F** is under construction and is projected to be the tallest building in sub-Saharan Africa ([Abidjan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abidjan)). **UNVERIFIED:** completion date and whether it will be topped out by 2028. Worth a sentence, not a paragraph.
- **The Cité Administrative** — the cluster of government towers in Plateau. **INCOMPLETE:** I was unable to source construction dates, architect, or visitor access within the research window. Treat as a drive-past landmark. **Note the photography prohibition** (§6.9) applies squarely here.
- **Is Plateau walkable?** Yes, in daylight, with the usual urban-crowd precautions. It is compact, gridded, and busy with office workers. It is the one part of Abidjan where a confident traveller could reasonably spend an hour on foot between the cathedral, the Place de la République and the lagoon front. Neither FCDO nor State advises against daytime walking. **Do not walk after dark** (FCDO, explicit) — which a port day makes moot.

### 5.3 Cocody and the Hôtel Ivoire

**Cocody** is the leafy, embassy-and-villa quarter north-east of Plateau, home to the university and the wealthiest residential districts. **7.8 km by road from the berth**, ~11 min free-flow, realistically 25–45 min.

**Hôtel Ivoire** (5.32604 N, 4.00415 W) is the landmark and a compressed history of the country:
- **Main building opened 7 September 1963**, designed by **Heinz Fenchel, Thomas Leitersdorf and William Pereira**.
- **24-storey tower added 1969**, **100 m (330 ft)** tall, 242 rooms, with **Le Toit d'Abidjan** restaurant on the 23rd floor — *"Abidjan's highest restaurant."* Further rooms added 1972.
- **The ice rink opened 1970** — *"considered the first of its kind in the West African region."* It decayed during the 1990s recession and **now functions only as a banquet hall**. **This is a very commonly repeated stale fact: the ice rink is gone.**
- **Cinema Ivoire reopened 2015**, 407 seats, Majestic One chain, the first 3D cinema in Côte d'Ivoire.
- **2004:** the hotel was the site of clashes between Gbagbo supporters and French UNOCI forces; **two demonstrators were killed**, and the hotel became symbolically bound to the Gbagbo regime.
- **InterContinental withdrew in 2002.** **Pierre Fakhoury** oversaw renovation **2009–2015**; an **Accor–Sofitel** management agreement began 2012; the refurbished hotel **reopened 15 May 2015 as Sofitel Abidjan Hôtel Ivoire**, 423 rooms, in Cocody's 8th arrondissement.
([Hôtel Ivoire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H%C3%B4tel_Ivoire))

**For a port day:** worth a stop for the view and a drink, or as a lunch venue on an excursion. Not a "sight" in itself. The grounds and lagoon frontage are pleasant and secure.

### 5.4 Treichville and the Marché de Treichville

**The closest neighbourhood to the ship — 2.7 km, and the port is literally in this commune.**

- Named for **Marcel Treich-Laplène (1860–1890)**, French colonial resident in Côte d'Ivoire. ([Treichville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treichville))
- *"Known as one of the most lively neighbourhoods in Abidjan, especially around the Crossroad France-Amérique."* Streets are **numbered 1–47 rather than named** — a small navigational quirk worth mentioning.
- Contains the **Palais de la Culture**, a swimming pool, sports park and racecourse; also the railway station with (nominal) passenger service toward Ouagadougou.
- Suffered significant violence and casualties during the **2010–2011 crisis**.

**The Grand Marché de Treichville** is the big covered market and the sensory heart of the district — textiles, wax prints, food, spices, plastic goods, music. It is exactly the *"crowded market"* environment the State Department flags for petty crime. **Go with a guide, carry a small amount of cash and nothing else, expect to be noticed, expect to bargain hard.** It is the single most authentic thing within walking distance of the ship — and "walking distance" is theoretical, because you cannot walk out of the port.

**INCOMPLETE:** I could not source current opening hours, or confirm the market's post-fire/renovation status. Treichville's market has burned more than once historically. **Verify before publication.**

### 5.5 Musée des Civilisations de Côte d'Ivoire — check before you promise it

Located in **Plateau, at the corner of Boulevards Nangui Abrogoua and Carde** ([Musée des Civilisations](https://www.museedescivilisations.com/)).

**Last verified hours and prices** ([museedescivilisations.com, Horaires et tarifs](https://www.museedescivilisations.com/horaires-d-ouverture/jours-heures/2-uncategorised/5-horaires-et-tarifs.html)):
- **Tuesday to Friday, 09:00–17:00.**
- **Saturday, Sunday and public holidays: by reservation, 72 hours in advance.**
- **Admission: 2,000 FCFA for foreign adults; 500 FCFA for Ivorian nationals.**

**The problem:** the **exhibition hall closed on Monday 9 September 2024 for rehabilitation**, with works planned over a **6-month period** — nominally reopening around March 2025. **The museum's own website returned HTTP 500 errors on every attempt during this research (30 July 2026), so I could not confirm current status.**

**MARKED UNVERIFIED:** whether the Musée des Civilisations is currently open, and whether the 2024 rehabilitation completed. **Do not promise it in the guide.** Write it as "check locally / confirm with the ship's shore excursion desk" and give the last known hours and prices with their date. Note that the museum is conspicuously **absent from every documented cruise excursion 2023–2026**, which is itself weak evidence that it has not been reliably available.

*(Note the naming trap: the **Musée National du Costume** is in **Grand-Bassam**, in the former Governor's Palace — a different institution, and the one that actually appears on cruise itineraries.)*

### 5.6 Banco National Park and the fanico laundry

**Parc National du Banco**, 5.39241 N, 4.05197 W. **13.4 km / 28 min free-flow from the berth**, realistically 45–75 min in traffic.

- **A genuine primary rainforest inside a city of six million.** Area **3,438 hectares**. ([Petit Futé, Office du Parc National du Banco](https://www.petitfute.co.uk/v63006-parc-national-du-banco/c1173-visites-points-d-interet/c974-site-naturel/194231-office-du-parc-national-du-banco.html))
- **Admission:** 1,000 FCFA nationals; **5,000 FCFA non-nationals**; 500 FCFA children. Guides available on site; walking and cycling on maintained trails; lunch can be arranged with at least a day's notice. (Same source.) **UNVERIFIED:** current opening hours, and whether these prices still hold — the source is a guidebook listing, not the park authority (OIPR). Treat prices as indicative.
- **The fanico laundry** — the reason to come. At the park's southern edge, in a stream valley beside the northern highway, hundreds of **fanicos** (laundrymen, historically largely of Burkinabè and Nigerien origin) wash clothes by hand against half-buried car tyres, and lay the washing out to dry across the hillside in vast blocks of colour. It is one of the most photographed sights in West Africa and one of the very few genuinely extraordinary things you can see in Abidjan in twenty minutes. The entrance is *"100 metres before the Shell station after passing the fanicos on the north highway."*
- **Does it work on a port day? Yes** — and it is on the documented cruise excursion menu (PAA cites *"la forêt du Banco"* for both *Seven Seas* calls). Allow 2.5–3.5 hours round trip from the ship including time on the ground.
- **Photography caution:** the fanicos are working people, not an exhibit. See §6.10.

### 5.7 Grand-Bassam — the standard excursion, and the right one

**39.4 km by road, 35 min free-flow, realistically 60–90 minutes each way.** Straight-line 32.6 km. (OSRM/Nominatim computation from the berth.)

*(Note: published distances vary — "about 42 km / ~1 hour", "40–45 minutes / ~40 km", "25 miles / 38 minutes", "approximately 30 km east of Abidjan". The variation comes from measuring between different points in two large municipalities. **39–42 km from the port is the right range; 30 km is too low.**)*

**What it is:**
- **First French colonial capital of Côte d'Ivoire, 1893–1900.** ([Grand-Bassam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand-Bassam))
- *"In **1899**, the colonial administration was transferred to the nearby town, **Bingerville**, after a devastating outbreak of **yellow fever**, which decimated the town's population."* — the same epidemic pressure that later pushed the French to Abidjan itself in 1898–1903. **The yellow fever certificate you need to enter the country is not an abstraction here; it is the reason this town is a ruin and not a capital.** That is the single best narrative hook in the whole port.
- **UNESCO World Heritage, inscribed 2012**, as the *"Historic Town of Grand-Bassam"*, recognised for outstanding examples of **colonial architecture and town planning** alongside the traditional **N'zima** (Nzema) settlement. ([UNESCO WHC List 1322](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1322/) — the UNESCO site is Cloudflare-protected and could not be fetched directly; inscription year and rationale corroborated via Wikipedia's Grand-Bassam article. **Verify criteria numbers before publication.**)
- **Ancien Bassam / Quartier France** is the colonial quarter: the former **Governor's Palace**, now the **Musée National du Costume**; the old post office, customs house, courthouse and merchant houses, many of them **still abandoned and roofless**, palms growing through the floors. It is beautiful and melancholy and completely unlike anything else on a world-cruise itinerary.
- The **beach** is long and Atlantic and the surf is dangerous (see §3.4 — **do not swim**).
- Craft and artisan markets operate along the approach road; this is where cruise passengers buy masks, bronzes, and printed cloth.

**The 2016 attack, stated plainly.** On **13 March 2016**, three gunmen affiliated with **al-Qaeda in the Islamic Maghreb (AQIM)** attacked beach hotels at Grand-Bassam, killing **19 people** — nine Ivorians, four French citizens, and one each Lebanese, German, Macedonian, Malian and Nigerian — and wounding at least 33. ([Grand-Bassam shootings; UN Security Council press statement SC/12279](https://press.un.org/en/2016/sc12279.doc.htm))

**Current security posture — the honest read:**
- **In December 2022, Ivorian courts handed down 11 life sentences** for the attack ([Al Jazeera, 28 Dec 2022](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2022/12/28/ivory-coast-hands-down-life-sentences-to-11-in-2016-resort-attack)).
- The state's response was structural: the **Northern Operational Zone (ZON)**, **CROAT** intelligence centre and the **AILCT** counterterrorism academy (US State Department advisory). Beach hotels at Bassam now have visible armed security and vehicle checks.
- **Neither the State Department nor FCDO advises against travel to Grand-Bassam.** It sits outside every restricted zone in both advisories.
- FCDO's standing terrorism language does specifically name *"beach resorts, hotels, cafés and restaurants"* as places where indiscriminate attacks could occur, and says attacks *"could occur anywhere, including major towns and cities such as Abidjan."* That is a general statement about the country, not a specific warning about Bassam.
- **There has been no repeat attack in the decade since.**

**Verdict:** Grand-Bassam is the standard cruise excursion, it is the right choice for a first-time visitor, and the 2016 attack should be told as history — factually, without either erasing it or letting it dominate. A guide that omits it is dishonest; a guide that leads with it is misleading.

### 5.8 Yamoussoukro and the Basilica — NOT feasible. Say so plainly.

**The brief's suspicion is correct.** Computed road distance from the berth to Yamoussoukro: **239.1 km**, free-flow driving time **2 h 51 min** one way (OSRM). Straight-line 218.3 km. The published figure of ~240 km is right; the "2 h 08 min at 112 km/h" figure circulating online assumes a motorway speed the A3 does not sustain end-to-end through towns.

**Realistic round trip: 6.5–8 hours of driving alone**, before any time at the basilica, before lunch, before getting out of the port and back through the gate. On a typical 08:00–17:00 or 07:00–18:00 port call this is **not possible**. Any operator offering it is offering a coach day with about forty minutes at the destination and a serious risk of missing the ship.

**What you would be missing** (for the guide's consolation paragraph):
- **Yamoussoukro was designated the political capital in 1983** by **Félix Houphouët-Boigny**, who chose his own birthplace ([Britannica, Abidjan](https://www.britannica.com/place/Abidjan)). The transfer has never fully happened: parliament, the Constitutional Council and the Supreme Court remain in Abidjan's Plateau, and **Abidjan is the "economic capital" and de facto seat of government** ([Abidjan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abidjan); [Ivory Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivory_Coast)).
- The **Basilique Notre-Dame de la Paix** was built **1985–1989** at a cost of about **US$300 million**, and is the largest church in the world by floor area — larger than St Peter's, on which it is modelled. John Paul II laid its foundation stone on his **1985** visit (the same trip on which he dedicated St Paul's in Abidjan) and consecrated it in **1990**, reportedly on condition that a hospital be built alongside. ([Basilica of Our Lady of Peace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilica_of_Our_Lady_of_Peace); [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Yamoussoukro-Basilica-of-Our-Lady-of-Peace-at-Yamoussoukro))
- **The Abidjan connection is real and worth making:** St Paul's Cathedral, which you *can* see in twenty minutes from the berth, was dedicated on the very day the basilica's foundation stone was laid. Tell that story instead of driving 480 km.

### 5.9 Also genuinely worth it

- **Bingerville Botanical Garden** — appears on three of four documented cruise excursions. Bingerville was the **second colonial capital (1900–1934)**, after Grand-Bassam and before Abidjan; the botanical garden and the old governor's residence are the draw. East of Cocody; roughly comparable driving time to Grand-Bassam. **INCOMPLETE:** hours, admission, current condition not sourced.
- **Village artisanal / craft markets** — cited in PAA's Zuiderdam release; the standard shopping stop.
- **The lagoon itself** — pirogue and ferry crossings are cheap (a lagoon ferry fare of around **150 FCFA** is reported by [Wikivoyage](https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Abidjan), which is dated — see CORRECTIONS) and the water-level view of the Plateau skyline is the best in the city. A ship-organised lagoon cruise, if offered, is a low-stress way to see Abidjan.
- **Cathédrale + Plateau + Treichville market** makes a coherent half-day for someone who does not want the long Bassam drive.

## 6. Practicalities

### 6.1 Currency

- **West African CFA franc**, ISO **XOF** (numeric 952). Issued by the **Banque Centrale des États de l'Afrique de l'Ouest (BCEAO)**, headquartered in Dakar. ([BCEAO](https://www.bceao.int/); [West African CFA franc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_African_CFA_franc))
- **Fixed parity: €1 = 655.957 XOF.** This is a hard peg, unchanged since the euro's introduction in 1999. BCEAO's own site displayed **655.957 CFA per euro on 30 July 2026**. There is no float and no exchange-rate risk against the euro.
- **Rule of thumb for USD:** at roughly $1 ≈ €0.92, expect **about 600 XOF to the dollar** — but the dollar rate moves; only the euro rate is fixed. **1,000 XOF ≈ €1.52 ≈ US$1.65.**
- **Eight member states:** Benin, Burkina Faso, **Côte d'Ivoire**, Guinea-Bissau, Mali, Niger, Senegal, Togo.
- **Notes:** 500, 1,000, 2,000, 5,000, 10,000 francs.
- **Not interchangeable** with the Central African CFA franc (XAF) despite the identical name and parity.
- **The "eco":** announced December 2019 as a renaming of the West African CFA with reduced French involvement; the wider ECOWAS single currency has a stated target of **July 2027**. **As of July 2026 the eco has not been introduced and the currency remains the XOF.** Treat 2027–2028 as a live uncertainty in a guide written for a 2028 call — but the peg and the notes in your hand will almost certainly still say CFA.
- **CORRECTION FLAG:** Wikipedia's West African CFA franc article states that France's guarantee remains contingent on central banks holding **at least 50% of foreign reserves at the French Treasury**, "as of March 2026". This appears **stale**. The 2019–2021 reform of the West African CFA specifically abolished the requirement to deposit reserves with the French Treasury and removed French representatives from BCEAO governance bodies, while retaining the euro peg and the French convertibility guarantee. **I could not retrieve the French Treasury's own page to confirm** (the URL 404s). **MARKED UNVERIFIED — do not repeat the 50% claim without checking the French Treasury or BCEAO directly.**

### 6.2 Cards, cash and ATMs

- **Côte d'Ivoire is a cash economy for a visitor.** Cards work at international hotels (Sofitel, Radisson, Pullman, Noom), some upscale restaurants in Plateau and Cocody, and airline offices. They do **not** work at markets, with taxis, at Banco park, at craft stalls, or in Grand-Bassam's old town.
- **ATMs** are widespread in Plateau, Cocody and Marcory, at bank branches (SGBCI, Ecobank, NSIA, BICICI, UBA). They dispense XOF. Visa is more widely accepted than Mastercard; Amex rarely.
- **FCDO's specific security instruction: "take transport to use ATMs or to exchange money at the bank"** — i.e. do not walk to or linger at an ATM. Use one inside a bank branch or hotel lobby, not a street-facing machine.
- **Card fraud:** FCDO — *"To prevent card fraud, keep your bank card in sight when paying bills."*
- **Best practice for a port day: bring euros in cash and change a small amount, or draw XOF from a bank ATM early in the day.** Euros are the natural currency to bring because of the fixed peg; USD is accepted for exchange but at a worse effective rate. **Do not change money on the street.**
- **How much?** For a ship-excursion day, 10,000–20,000 XOF (€15–30) covers drinks, tips and small purchases. For independent movement with taxis and a market, 30,000–50,000 XOF.
- **Leftover XOF is nearly worthless outside the eight-country zone** — spend it or keep a note as a souvenir.

### 6.3 Language

- **French is the sole official language** ([Ivory Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivory_Coast)). Around 60–70 indigenous languages are spoken; **Dioula** is the main lingua franca of trade, and **Nouchi**, the Abidjan street argot (which gave the world the words *coupé* and *décalé*), is the youth vernacular.
- **English is not widely spoken.** This is one of the least English-friendly ports on a world cruise. Hotel staff at international properties, some guides, and airline and bank staff will manage; taxi drivers, market traders and park staff generally will not. FCDO maintains a list of medical providers *"where some staff will speak English"* — the phrasing tells you how scarce it is.
- **Practical consequence:** an independent day without French is genuinely harder here than in almost any other African port a world cruise visits. This is a substantive argument for the ship excursion, not a soft one. Carry the ship's name, the berth and a return time written in French; screenshot a map.
- A few words go a long way: *bonjour*, *merci*, *s'il vous plaît*, *combien ?* ("how much?"), *c'est trop cher* ("too expensive"), *non merci*.

### 6.4 Taxis — the orange/woro-woro distinction

Two systems, and telling them apart matters:

- **Orange (also described as orange/red) metered taxis** — the city-wide, hail-anywhere taxis. They may cross communes and go anywhere in Abidjan. Meters (*compteurs*) exist and are legally required, but **in practice drivers frequently refuse to use them with foreigners and quote a flat fare instead**. **Agree the fare before you get in.** Wikivoyage's guidance — "expensive, negotiate beforehand" — matches every other account ([Wikivoyage Abidjan](https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Abidjan)).
- **Woro-woro** — colour-coded **shared** taxis that operate on fixed routes **within a single commune** (each commune has its own colour: yellow in Cocody, green in Yopougon, and so on). They are cheap, they fill up with strangers, and they will not take you across the city. **Not a realistic option for a cruise passenger**, both for language and for the fact that they cannot get you back into the port. Worth explaining in the guide purely so passengers understand what they are seeing.
- **Gbaka** — the battered minibuses. Not for visitors.
- **Ride-hailing apps** operate in Abidjan (Yango and Uber have both operated in the market). **FCDO explicitly recommends them over street taxis** for vehicle condition. They require a working data connection and a pickup point — which the port gate complicates.
- **Fares — treat as indicative, not sourced.** Wikivoyage cites 2,500–5,000 XOF for an airport taxi run. **MARKED UNVERIFIED:** current metered tariffs and typical Plateau/Bassam fares. Do not print a fare table. The honest advice is: agree the price before boarding, have small notes (drivers rarely have change for a 10,000), and expect a foreigner premium.

### 6.5 Tipping

**No strong sourced data.** Ivorian practice broadly follows French convention: service is often included (*service compris*) in restaurants, and rounding up or leaving 5–10% for good service is appreciated rather than expected. Guides and drivers on cruise excursions **do** expect a tip and the amounts are effectively set by cruise-passenger convention (roughly €5–10 per person for a half-day guide, €3–5 for a driver). Small notes in XOF are far more useful than foreign coins, which cannot be exchanged. **MARKED UNVERIFIED / customary practice, not sourced.**

### 6.6 Mobile connectivity

- Three mobile operators serve Côte d'Ivoire: **Orange Côte d'Ivoire, MTN Côte d'Ivoire, and Moov Africa**, regulated by **ARTCI** (Autorité de Régulation des Télécommunications/TIC de Côte d'Ivoire). **INCOMPLETE:** I could not retrieve ARTCI's observatory data (URL 404) for subscriber numbers or penetration, and I have not verified the operator list against a primary regulator source within the research window. **Verify against [artci.ci](https://www.artci.ci/) before publication.**
- **4G coverage in Abidjan is good**; 5G has been deployed in parts of the city.
- **SIM registration is mandatory** in Côte d'Ivoire (identity document required), which makes a same-day tourist SIM a nuisance for a port call.
- **Practical advice for a port day:** use an **eSIM** bought before arrival, or accept roaming charges, or simply go without data and rely on the excursion. Do not plan on buying a local SIM in a few hours.
- **Note FCDO's warning that internet access might be restricted in the event of significant unrest** — a reason not to make your only re-boarding plan dependent on a phone.

### 6.7 Dress

- **Modest and practical.** FCDO: *"Dress modestly when visiting religious places or buildings."* For St Paul's Cathedral: shoulders and knees covered.
- Abidjan is a **hot, humid, coastal city** — light natural fibres, a hat, sunglasses, and closed comfortable shoes for the port apron and market floors. Avoid obvious beachwear away from the beach.
- **Do not display wealth.** FCDO and State both say it: no visible expensive watches or jewellery. A world-cruise passenger walking a Treichville market in a Rolex is the specific scenario both advisories are written about.
- Ivorians dress well and take appearance seriously; scruffy tourists are noticed.

### 6.8 LGBT+ travellers

FCDO: *"Same-sex sexual activity is not illegal in Côte d'Ivoire, but the law does not recognise same-sex partnerships or marriage. Attitudes are less tolerant and there is no specific anti-discrimination law to protect LGBT+ people."* ([FCDO Safety and security](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/cote-d-ivoire/safety-and-security)) Not criminalised — which distinguishes Côte d'Ivoire from many neighbours — but public discretion is advisable.

### 6.9 Photography restrictions — get this right

**FCDO's list is explicit and should be reproduced verbatim in the guide.** It is *prohibited* to take photographs near sensitive installations, including:
- **military sites**
- **government buildings**
- **radio and TV stations**
- **the Presidency building**
- **Abidjan Airport**
([FCDO Safety and security](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/cote-d-ivoire/safety-and-security))

**Points of caution and correction:**
- **"Government buildings"** is broad and includes the Cité Administrative towers and ministries in Plateau — precisely the skyline a passenger will want to photograph. Photographing the skyline generally from the lagoon or from a moving vehicle is normal; standing at the base of a ministry with a long lens is not. Uniformed personnel have wide discretion and a confiscated phone is a bad way to spend a port day.
- **Bridges are widely stated online to be prohibited.** They are **not** on FCDO's list. **MARKED UNVERIFIED.** Given that the Houphouët-Boigny and Charles de Gaulle bridges carry military and government traffic and adjoin the port, discretion is sensible regardless — but do not state a prohibition that no official source supports.
- **The port itself.** The port is a **restricted customs enclosure under ISPS security** with its own Département Sûreté, gendarmerie and police commissariat ([PAA Capitainerie](https://www.portabidjan.ci/fr/offre-de-service/la-capitainerie)). Photography of port infrastructure, security posts and gates should be assumed unwelcome even though FCDO does not name ports. Photograph your own ship, not the security gate.

### 6.10 Photographing people

**The standard advice applies here with more force than usual.** Ask first, every time. Many Ivorians will refuse; some will ask for money; a few will react angrily to a camera raised without permission. At the **Banco fanico laundry** in particular — the most photogenic scene in the city — the men working there are doing hard, poorly paid manual labour and are photographed constantly. A guide will negotiate access and a customary payment on your behalf. Photographing children without a parent's consent is not acceptable. Do not photograph police, soldiers, checkpoints or anyone in uniform.

### 6.11 Time, electricity, emergency numbers

- **Time zone: GMT (UTC+0), no daylight saving.** In April, that is UTC+0 while much of Europe is on UTC+1/+2 — worth a line for anyone calling home.
- **Electricity: 230 V, 50 Hz, French type C/E plugs.** *(Standard for the region; not separately sourced here.)*
- **Ambulance: 185** ([FCDO Health](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/cote-d-ivoire/health)). Police 170/111, fire 180 — **UNVERIFIED**; the ship's number matters more than any of these.
- **The number that actually matters is the ship's port agent**, printed on the daily programme. Carry it.

Abidjan is never dry in the way that word is used in temperate climates; "dry season" here means "less rain", not "low humidity".

### 7.2 The seasons, precisely

Abidjan has a **tropical monsoon climate with a bimodal rainfall regime** — two wet seasons and two drier ones:

1. **Long dry season: December – February/early March.** Least rain (Jan 15 mm), most sun, hottest afternoons. This is the classic "best time to visit".
2. **Long rainy season: March/April – July**, peaking violently in **June (375 mm, 21 rain days)**. May and June are the wettest months by a wide margin.
3. **Short dry season / "little dry season": August – September.** Rainfall drops sharply (August 50 mm) but this is the **cloudiest and coolest** stretch of the year — the coastal upwelling drops sea temperature to 25 °C and afternoon maxima to 27.7 °C, and skies stay grey. Locally this cool, overcast, low-rain period is a distinct season in its own right.
4. **Short rainy season: October – November**, moderate (110–150 mm), then tapering into December.

**FCDO's simpler framing** for flood-risk purposes: *"The rainy season is between May and November. Torrential rains often cause flooding, landslides and large potholes. Flash flooding has caused deaths."* ([FCDO Safety and security](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/cote-d-ivoire/safety-and-security)). **This is a hazard-framing, not a climatological one, and it disagrees with the rainfall data** (March and April are already wet; August is not). For a guide, use the four-season climatological framing and quote FCDO's May–November window as the flood-risk period.

### 7.3 Harmattan

**Yes, it reaches Abidjan — but weakly.**

The **harmattan** is the dry, dust-laden north-easterly that blows off the Sahara across West Africa during the northern winter, **roughly December to February**. Britannica describes it as *"the dust-laden wind [that] blows in the dry period"* in Côte d'Ivoire ([Britannica, Côte d'Ivoire — Climate](https://www.britannica.com/place/Cote-dIvoire/Climate)).

**But Climates to Travel notes it *"appears less frequently in Abidjan than in interior regions of Ivory Coast"*** — the coastal moisture and 500+ km of forest between the Sahel and the lagoon blunt it. When it does reach the coast, the effects are haze that flattens the light and cuts visibility, notably drier air, cooler nights, and irritated eyes and sinuses.

**For a 2028 call in April, harmattan is not a factor.** It is out of season.

### 7.4 What to expect on the 2028 call — and when it will be

**Working out the month.** Holland America's announced routing for the 2028 Grand World Voyage on *Volendam* is, in order: depart **Fort Lauderdale 4 January 2028** → Caribbean → east coast of South America → **Antarctica (4-day scenic Experience)** → Beagle Channel, Glacier Alley, Chilean Fjords → **first equator crossing** → **Easter Island** → South Pacific and French Polynesia (Bora Bora) → **eight days, six New Zealand ports** → Australia → Indonesia → **Singapore** → **Sri Lanka** → round the **Cape of Good Hope** → **65 hours in Cape Town** → **second equator crossing** → **Gulf of Guinea including a sail-past of Null Island (0°N, 0°E)** → **West African ports** → transatlantic → **San Juan** → **Fort Lauderdale**. 129 days; **45 ports, 26 countries and territories, six continents**; **16 Mariners' Collection ports**, which include **Abidjan, Takoradi, Cape Town and Walvis Bay**; six overnights (Rio, Tahiti, Auckland, Sydney, Singapore, Cape Town). ([HAL press release, 30 April 2026](https://www.hollandamerica.com/en/us/news/2026/April/news-04302026-grands-2028); [HAL Grand World Voyage page](https://www.hollandamerica.com/en/us/cruise-destinations/grand-voyages-world), voyage code **W8W129**; [PR Newswire, bookings open](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/holland-america-line-opens-bookings-for-2028-grand-voyages-302778112.html))

**129 days from 4 January 2028 ends about 11–12 May 2028.** West Africa sits on the **final leg**, after Cape Town and before the Atlantic crossing to San Juan (7–9 days). Working backwards from a ~11 May return, with a San Juan call and the Atlantic crossing consuming the last ~10–12 days, **the West African string falls in roughly the last week of April into the first days of May 2028.**

**Corroborating signal:** CruiseMapper's Abidjan port page shows month placeholders including **May 2028** for future calls ([CruiseMapper Abidjan](https://www.cruisemapper.com/ports/abidjan-port-721)) — consistent with a very late April / early May call.

> **CORRECTION to the brief's assumption.** The brief anticipated "a westbound Africa leg in roughly February–April". The published routing is **eastbound-then-northbound**: the ship reaches Africa *from the Indian Ocean*, rounds the Cape, and works **north up the west coast** at the *end* of the voyage. **Abidjan is a late-April-to-early-May 2028 call, not a February–March one.** The exact date is **UNVERIFIED** — HAL had published no dated per-port schedule for W8W129 as of 30 July 2026.

**What that means for weather.** Late April / early May is **the ramp into the main rainy season** and squarely the **hottest and most humid** part of the year:

- **Air temperature:** highs **31–32 °C**, lows **25 °C**. April has the year's highest minimum temperature (25.2 °C) — **nights barely cool down**.
- **Humidity:** high, around 80%. Combined with 32 °C this gives a heat index well above 38 °C. **This is the operative fact for a world-cruise demographic.** Heat exhaustion, not crime, is the most likely thing to spoil a passenger's day at Abidjan.
- **Rain:** April 145 mm over 11 days; May 235 mm over 17 days. **Expect at least one heavy downpour**, typically a short, violent afternoon convective storm rather than all-day drizzle. Streets flood fast and drain slowly; a Grand-Bassam return in a downpour can take much longer than planned.
- **Sun:** 6–7 hours a day; UV is extreme at 5°N.
- **Sea:** 29 °C.

**Practical packing line for the guide:** light long sleeves (sun and mosquitoes both), a genuinely waterproof light shell or a folding umbrella, a hat, high-factor sunscreen, DEET repellent, closed shoes that tolerate a wet street, and **more water than you think** — at least 1.5 litres carried, and a plan to buy sealed bottled water rather than ration.

## 8. History and context

### 8.1 The lagoon peoples — and the naming question

The original inhabitants of the Abidjan lagoon region are an **Akan** people whose own name for themselves is **Tchaman** (also written **Atchan**, **Kyaman**, **Gyaman**), meaning approximately **"the chosen ones"** in their own language. The name by which they are more commonly known — **Ébrié** — **was given to them by the neighbouring Abouré people, in whose language it means "bad"**, applied after a military defeat. ([Tchaman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tchaman))

**Currently correct usage, for the guide:** *Tchaman* (or *Atchan*) is the endonym and the respectful form; *Ébrié* is the exonym, is pejorative in origin, and survives mainly in the name of the **Ébrié Lagoon** and in older literature. **Best practice: "the Tchaman (Atchan) people, historically called Ébrié"** — and keep "Ébrié Lagoon" as the geographic name, which is universal and unavoidable.

Their oral history holds that they came from the north-east, near Ashanti lands in present-day Ghana, and fled south after a defeat. Their territory runs along the lagoon **from Grand-Bassam in the east to Assagni in the west**, encompassing Abidjan. **Atchan** is a **Kwa** language, still spoken in and around the city.

**The city's name.** The traditional etymology: a Frenchman asked a Tchaman man the name of the place; the man, misunderstanding, answered *"Mɛn chan ábijan"* — "I was just cutting leaves/grass" — and the Frenchman wrote down *Abidjan*. ([Tchaman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tchaman)) It is a folk etymology of the classic colonial-misunderstanding type and should be told as such — charming, widely repeated, unprovable.

### 8.2 French colonisation and why Abidjan grew

- **Grand-Bassam** was the first French colonial capital, **1893–1900**. ([Grand-Bassam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand-Bassam))
- **1899:** a devastating **yellow fever** epidemic decimated Grand-Bassam and the administration moved to **Bingerville**.
- **1898:** French colonists relocated to the Abidjan site, driven by the same epidemics ([Abidjan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abidjan)). Britannica has Abidjan as *"a village in 1898"* becoming a town in **1903**, and developing as the **rail terminus from 1904** ([Britannica, Abidjan](https://www.britannica.com/place/Abidjan)).
- **The railway is the reason Abidjan exists as a city.** The line inland toward Bouaké and eventually Ouagadougou needed a coastal terminus, and Abidjan's sheltered lagoon position — safe from the surf that made Grand-Bassam and Port-Bouët miserable anchorages — made it the choice.
- **Colonial capital: 1933** per Wikipedia; **1934** per Britannica, succeeding Bingerville. **Sources disagree by one year — report both.**

### 8.3 The Vridi Canal, 1950 — the hinge of the whole story

Everything above was constrained by one fact: **the lagoon had no navigable outlet to the sea.** Ships lay off an open surf coast and cargo was worked by lighter and by wharf — slow, dangerous, and a hard ceiling on how big the colony's export economy could get.

Two attempts to cut through the sand barrier, **west of Port-Bouët near Petit-Bassam in 1906 and 1907, failed** — littoral drift closed them almost immediately. It took the engineer **Roger Pelnard-Considère** (design 1932–33, model-tested at **Delft**, approved **December 1935**) to work out a cut oriented to resist the drift. Construction from **1938**; the **last plug blasted on 23 July 1950**; the port formally opened **5 February 1951**, inaugurated by **François Mitterrand** as French Minister for Overseas Territories. First ship through: *Marie Dominique*; first ocean-going vessel: *Volta*. ([PAA, Historique](https://www.portabidjan.ci/fr/autorite-portuaire/historique))

**What it did to the city:** it turned a lagoon-side rail terminus into a deepwater port overnight. Britannica: the canal's opening *"enabled Abidjan to emerge as the major shipping and financial centre of French-speaking West Africa"* ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Abidjan)). Wikipedia: the city *"gained major port capabilities after the Vridi Canal's completion in 1950, transforming it into West Africa's financial centre by the late 1950s"* ([Abidjan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abidjan)). Cocoa, coffee, timber, bananas, pineapples and manganese flowed out; the population exploded; the modernist skyline of Plateau followed in the 1960s and 70s.

**The narrative line for the guide:** *your ship enters Abidjan through a hole a French engineer blew in a sandbar in 1950, and that hole is the reason there is a city here worth visiting.*

### 8.4 Independence and Houphouët-Boigny

- **Independence: 7 August 1960.** **Félix Houphouët-Boigny** — physician, planter, French cabinet minister, and founder of the Rassemblement Démocratique Africain — became the first president and governed until his death in **1993**.
- The **"Ivorian miracle"** of the 1960s–70s: cocoa and coffee wealth, heavy French investment and an expatriate French community, the towers of Plateau, the Hôtel Ivoire with its ice rink, La Pyramide. Abidjan acquired its "Paris of West Africa" tag in this period and has never quite lost it.
- **1983: Houphouët-Boigny designated Yamoussoukro, his birthplace, as the political capital.** ([Britannica, Abidjan](https://www.britannica.com/place/Abidjan)) The move has never been completed: parliament, the Constitutional Council and the Supreme Court all remain in Abidjan's Plateau.
- **Formal status today: Yamoussoukro is the political/administrative capital; Abidjan is the economic capital and the de facto seat of government.** ([Abidjan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abidjan); [Ivory Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivory_Coast)) The guide should use exactly this formulation — the "which is the capital?" question is a reliable point of confusion.
- **1990:** the **Basilique Notre-Dame de la Paix** completed at Yamoussoukro (1985–1989, ~US$300m). Houphouët-Boigny died in 1993 and is buried there.

### 8.5 First Ivorian Civil War, 2002–2007

([First Ivorian Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Ivorian_Civil_War))

- **Root cause:** a prolonged succession crisis after Houphouët-Boigny's 1993 death, and the spread of **"nativist and xenophobic discourse"** — the ideology of **ivoirité**, a nationalist doctrine used by successive leaders to define who counted as authentically Ivorian, and to marginalise northern Muslims, immigrants from Burkina Faso and Mali (whose labour built the cocoa economy), and above all the opposition politician **Alassane Ouattara**, whose eligibility was repeatedly challenged on grounds of parentage.
- **19 September 2002:** roughly **750 troops**, mostly northerners, attacked three cities simultaneously. They failed to take Abidjan but seized most of the **north**. The country split: government south, rebel **Forces Nouvelles** north under **Guillaume Soro**, against President **Laurent Gbagbo**.
- **22 September 2002:** France launched **Opération Licorne**, initially to protect foreign nationals. **UN peacekeepers (UNOCI) deployed from April 2004.**
- **6 November 2004 — the rupture with France.** Ivorian aircraft bombed the French base at **Bouaké, killing nine French soldiers**. France responded by **destroying the entire Ivorian air force** on the ground within hours. **Massive anti-French riots followed in Abidjan**, including at the Hôtel Ivoire, where French forces and Gbagbo supporters clashed and two demonstrators were killed. Thousands of French and other foreign nationals were evacuated. This is the event that ended Abidjan's status as an expatriate city and it still colours attitudes.
- **Peace accords:** Linas-Marcoussis (2003), then the **Ouagadougou Political Agreement, 4 March 2007**, which formally ended the war and made Soro prime minister.

### 8.6 Second Ivorian Civil War, 2010–2011

([Second Ivorian Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Ivorian_Civil_War))

- **November 2010 presidential election.** The **Independent Electoral Commission declared Alassane Ouattara the winner**. The **Constitutional Council**, headed by a Gbagbo ally, **annulled results in northern regions and declared Gbagbo the winner**. Both were sworn in. The UN, ECOWAS, the AU and effectively the entire international community recognised **Ouattara**.
- Months of standoff, sanctions and sporadic violence gave way to war. **28 March 2011:** Ouattara's **Republican Forces (FRCI)** launched a nationwide offensive and swept south in days.
- **The Battle of Abidjan began 31 March 2011.** Some **50,000 personnel deserted Gbagbo's forces**, leaving roughly 2,000 loyalists. The presidential palace fell by **5 April**. UN and French forces conducted strikes on heavy weapons.
- **The Duékoué massacre**, in the west, killed **at least 800** by the ICRC's count, with estimates above 1,000; the UN attributed many of the deaths to pro-Ouattara forces. **Atrocities were committed by both sides** — this must not be written as a clean liberation narrative.
- **11 April 2011:** French forces assisted FRCI troops in storming Gbagbo's residence; **Gbagbo was captured unharmed** and held at the Golf Hotel.
- **Toll: approximately 3,000 dead; over 100,000 refugees.**
- **Aftermath:** Gbagbo was transferred to the **International Criminal Court**, tried for crimes against humanity, and **acquitted in 2019** (upheld on appeal 2021). He returned to Côte d'Ivoire in 2021. Simone Gbagbo, his former wife, stood in the 2025 presidential election.
- **Treichville — the commune your ship berths in — saw significant violence and casualties in this period** ([Treichville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treichville)).

**Fifteen years on**, the physical city is rebuilt and the economy has been among Africa's fastest-growing. The politics, as §2.5 records, remain contested.

### 8.7 Population — current figures, with the right caveats

**The authoritative figure is the national census, RGPH 2021.** Enumeration ran **8 November – 14 December 2021**; definitive results were published by the **Ministère du Plan et du Développement / Institut National de la Statistique**, announced **7 July 2022** and detailed thereafter ([abidjan.net report of the definitive results](https://news.abidjan.net/articles/710138/resultats-globaux-definitifs-du-rgph-2021-la-population-vivant-habituellement-sur-le-territoire-ivoirien-se-chiffre-a-29-389-150-habitants); [ANSTAT publication](https://www.anstat.ci/); [Ministère du Plan](https://plan.gouv.ci/accueil/actualite/652)):

- **National resident population: 29,389,150.**
- **District Autonome d'Abidjan: approximately 6,329,021 — 21.5% of the national population.** (Wikipedia's Abidjan article rounds this to **6.3 million**, making it *"the sixth most populous city proper in Africa"*.)
- **Urbanisation: 52.5% urban (15,428,957) / 47.5% rural (13,960,193).**
- A post-census survey (17 March – 10 April 2022) estimated a **4.6% national omission rate** — i.e. the true figure is somewhat higher than the published count.
- **Area of the District Autonome: 2,119 km²**, comprising **10 central communes** (Abobo, Adjamé, Attécoubé, Cocody, Plateau in the north; Koumassi, Marcory, Port-Bouët, Treichville and part of Yopougon in the south) **plus 4 outlying sub-prefectures** ([Abidjan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abidjan)).

**Caveats a careful guide must carry:**
- **The census is from 2021.** With growth around 3% a year, the District's 2026 population is plausibly **7.2–7.5 million**, and by the 2028 call **around 7.5–8 million** — but **that is extrapolation, not a sourced figure. Do not print it as fact.**
- **UN-derived "urban agglomeration" series give lower numbers** — e.g. **6,057,000 for 2025** ([Macrotrends](https://www.macrotrends.net/global-metrics/cities/21602/abidjan/population)) — which is *below* the 2021 census count. The discrepancy is a **definitional** one (UN agglomeration boundaries vs. the Ivorian administrative District) compounded by the UN series lagging the census. **Cite the census, name it as the District Autonome, and give the date. Do not average the two.**
- **A new census (RGPH 2026) may be in preparation. UNVERIFIED.**

**National context:** population estimated at **31.5 million (2024)**; nominal GDP **US$79.43 bn (2023)**, GDP per capita **US$2,728**; average growth **7.1% a year 2012–2023, second-fastest in Africa**. Religion at the 2021 census: **Islam 42.5%, Christianity 39.8%, no religion 12.6%, traditional 2.2%, other 0.7%**. ([Ivory Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivory_Coast))

## 9. Food, culture, music

### 9.1 Food a visitor will actually encounter

([Ivorian cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivorian_cuisine))

- **Attiéké** — **the national dish and the one to try.** Fermented cassava pulp, grated and rolled into tiny couscous-like granules, steamed. Mild, faintly sour, slightly granular. Served as the starch alongside grilled fish or chicken, with raw onion and tomato and a chilli condiment. Almost always accompanied by **piment** (scotch bonnet paste) or a Maggi cube dissolved into the sauce — Maggi is genuinely ubiquitous in Ivorian cooking and is not a shortcut but part of the flavour vocabulary.
- **Garba** — **the Abidjan street food.** Attiéké with fried tuna, onion, tomato, chilli, sold from roadside stalls (*garbadromes*) run largely by Nigerien migrants. Cheap, fast, greasy, beloved, and the thing every Abidjanais will name if you ask what to eat. **Also, honestly, the highest-risk food for a visitor's stomach** — street-fried fish held at ambient temperature in a humid climate. Try it if you are adventurous and it is freshly fried in front of you; skip it if you have a delicate constitution and a ship to catch.
- **Kedjenou** — the dish to order in a restaurant. Chicken (or guinea fowl) with tomato, onion, ginger, garlic and chilli, **sealed in a clay pot called a *canari*** and cooked slowly over low heat with almost no added liquid, so the meat steams in its own juices. Traditionally the pot is shaken rather than stirred. Deep, concentrated, not fiery. **The single best thing you will eat in Côte d'Ivoire.**
- **Alloco** — ripe plantain cut into chunks and **deep-fried in palm oil**, served with a spicy onion-and-chilli relish, often with hard-boiled egg, sometimes with grilled fish. Sold everywhere on the street from late afternoon. Sweet, salty, hot, excellent.
- **Foutou / foufou** — plantain and yam (or cassava) pounded together into a smooth, elastic paste, torn off by hand and dipped in sauce. **Foutou banane** is beaten until it stretches.
- **Sauce graine** — the classic accompaniment to foutou: palm nuts crushed to a paste, cooked out with fish or meat, topped with brilliant red palm oil. Rich, distinctive, an acquired taste worth acquiring.
- **Placali** — fermented cassava paste, sourer than foutou, eaten the same way.
- **Sauce arachide** (groundnut) and **sauce claire** round out the standard menu.
- **Drinks:** **bangui** (palm wine, fermented and served fresh); **bissap** (hibiscus infusion, deep red, tart, sweetened); **gnamakoudji / nyamanku** (ginger juice — pulped ginger strained through cloth, mellowed with pineapple, lemon and vanilla, non-alcoholic and fierce); locally brewed lagers (Flag, Bock, Ivoire); and, of course, coffee.
- **Practical warning:** all of the above collides with §3.4. On a port day, eat at a hotel or an established restaurant, or on the ship's excursion, if you want to be certain. **Bottled drinks, no ice, hot food hot.**

### 9.2 Coffee and cocoa

**Cocoa is the country's defining industry and one of the genuine reasons to be interested in Côte d'Ivoire.**

- **Côte d'Ivoire is the world's largest producer and exporter of cocoa beans**, and has been for decades ([Ivory Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivory_Coast); [Cocoa production in Ivory Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cocoa_production_in_Ivory_Coast)).
- **World production, 2024/25: 4.723 million tonnes** — ICCO's revised estimate, published in the **Quarterly Bulletin of Cocoa Statistics, Issue No. 2, Volume LII, Cocoa Year 2025/26, released 29 May 2026**. That was **up 361,000 t (+8.3%)** on 2023/24, while **grindings fell to 4.628 Mt (−182,000 t, −3.8%)**; end-of-season stocks **1.320 Mt**, stocks-to-grindings **28.5%**. ([ICCO May 2026 QBCS](https://www.icco.org/may-2026-quarterly-bulletin-of-cocoa-statistics/))
- **Côte d'Ivoire's own share — handle with care.** The Ivorian government forecast **2.1–2.2 million tonnes for 2024/25**, up ~25% on the previous season ([USDA FAS, *Côte d'Ivoire — Cocoa Sector Overview 2025*](https://apps.fas.usda.gov/newgainapi/api/Report/DownloadReportByFileName?fileName=Cote+d%27Ivoire+-+Cocoa+Sector+Overview+-+2025_Accra_Cote+d%27Ivoire_IV2025-0001.pdf)). Against ICCO's 4.723 Mt world total, **2.1–2.2 Mt is roughly 44–47% of world production.**
- **The contradictory figures you will see online: "45%" and "about one-third".** Both circulate. The **~45%** figure is consistent with the government forecast over the ICCO world total; the **"about one-third"** figure appears in reporting that either uses a different season, uses exports rather than production, or is simply stale from a low-crop year. **The defensible statement for the guide: "Côte d'Ivoire is the world's largest cocoa producer, growing roughly 40–45% of the global crop — about 2.1–2.2 million tonnes in the 2024/25 season, against world production of 4.72 million tonnes."** Give both the numerator and the denominator with their sources; do not print a bare percentage.
- **ICCO does not publish country splits in its free press releases**, so the country figure above is government-forecast rather than ICCO-verified. **Flagged.**
- **Coffee:** Côte d'Ivoire was once among the world's largest robusta producers — third globally in the 1970s–80s — and coffee built the early post-independence economy alongside cocoa. Output has fallen very substantially since. **UNVERIFIED:** current coffee production and world ranking.
- **The uncomfortable part, which an honest guide includes:** cocoa is grown overwhelmingly by smallholders on very low incomes; child labour in the sector is extensively documented; and cocoa expansion has driven severe deforestation — **Banco National Park's 3,438 hectares survive as a fragment of a forest that once covered most of the south.** The EU Deforestation Regulation and various industry traceability schemes are reshaping the trade. This is not a scold; it is context that makes the chocolate you buy at the craft market mean something.

### 9.3 Music

- **Zouglou** emerged around **1990** from student protest culture at the University of Abidjan (Cocody) — hardship, satire, social commentary, sung over a percussive dance rhythm. **Magic System**'s *"Premier Gaou"* (1999) took it worldwide. Zouglou is the older, more explicitly political of Côte d'Ivoire's two great exports.
- **Coupé-décalé** is the other, and the one you will hear on every street. ([Coupé-décalé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coup%C3%A9-d%C3%A9cal%C3%A9))
  - **It was born in Paris, not Abidjan** — in the **early 2000s**, among the Ivorian diaspora at the **Atlantis nightclub in north-eastern Paris**, where *atalakus* (hype men) worked the crowd.
  - **Douk Saga** led the **Jet Set** collective and is credited with launching it; his **2003** track **"Sagacité"** was *"the first hit of the genre."*
  - **DJ Arafat** drove the second wave with a harder, more digital sound and became the genre's biggest star until his death in 2019 — his funeral in Abidjan drew enormous crowds and turned into a national event.
  - **The name is Nouchi slang:** ***coupé*** = to cheat/swindle, ***décalé*** = to run away/dodge. Together: beat the system and get out — a diaspora hustler's creed, half joke and half autobiography.
  - **Its rise is inseparable from the war.** Coupé-décalé became *"a dominant cultural force"* during the **2002** civil war: under curfews and with the country cut in half, Ivorian youth *"gathered in local bars and nightclubs to dance and celebrate resilience."* It is escapist music made in a country that had a great deal to escape from, and its flamboyance — designer labels, cash thrown at the DJ, **La Sape** dress culture — is the point, not a decoration.
  - **Musically:** percussive, minimal, electronic, drawing on Congolese **ndombolo** and **kwassa kwassa**, Ivorian zouglou, Cameroonian **makossa**, and Caribbean forms.
- **Also worth naming:** **Alpha Blondy** and **Tiken Jah Fakoly**, two of Africa's most important reggae artists, both Ivorian, both explicitly political. Alpha Blondy sang in Dioula, French and English and was a genuine continental star from the 1980s.

## 10. What is genuinely worth it — and what to skip

### 10.1 The honest recommendation

**Take the ship's excursion. This is one of the ports where that is the right answer rather than the lazy one.** The reasons are specific and stack:

1. **You cannot walk out.** The berth sits inside a customs enclosure where public access to the quays is prohibited by regulation and requires a pass from the Port Commander ([PAA Capitainerie](https://www.portabidjan.ci/fr/offre-de-service/la-capitainerie)). There is no terminal, no taxi rank, no walk-out gate. Independent movement requires transport that has been cleared into the port — which is exactly what an excursion is.
2. **The visa position is unresolved for individuals.** A visa is formally required; the e-visa is valid only at the airport (§4). Ship excursions travel under the vessel's collective arrangement. Independent passengers may or may not be covered, and **you will not find out until the morning of the call**.
3. **Language.** French only, and not much English (§6.3). Abidjan is harder to improvise in without French than almost any other African port on a world-cruise route.
4. **The best things are not walkable anyway.** Grand-Bassam is 39 km away; Banco is 13 km; even the cathedral is 4 km across a bridge. There is no "old town by the pier" here.
5. **Traffic risk to the ship's departure.** Abidjan traffic is genuinely bad and a late-April downpour makes it worse. On a ship excursion, the ship waits. Independently, it does not.

### 10.2 When independent exploration *is* reasonable

For a specific kind of traveller, and with preparation:
- **You speak functional French.**
- **You have confirmed with the ship, in writing, that independent shore leave is permitted and that there is transport through the gate.**
- **You have pre-booked a driver-guide** through a reputable Abidjan operator, who meets you at the gate with your name and your ship's name, at an agreed all-in price in XOF, for an agreed itinerary and a hard return time at least two hours before all-aboard.
- **You are back well before dark.**

That is a genuinely better day than a coach — you can spend an hour at the fanico laundry instead of fifteen minutes, and eat kedjenou somewhere real. But it is **pre-booked private touring, not spontaneous independence.** Turning up at the gangway intending to find a taxi and wing it is the one approach that does not work at Abidjan.

### 10.3 The best single-day itineraries

**If it is your first and probably only visit — the classic (7–8 hours):**
St Paul's Cathedral (45 min) → drive through Plateau for the skyline and La Pyramide → **Grand-Bassam** (2.5–3 hours: Quartier France, the Musée National du Costume, the beach — looked at, not swum in, and the craft market) → lunch → back. This is what every line sells and it is, honestly, the right shape.

**If you would rather see the living city than the dead colonial one (5–6 hours):**
St Paul's Cathedral → **Banco National Park and the fanico laundry** (the single most extraordinary sight in Abidjan) → **Treichville Grand Marché** with a guide → Hôtel Ivoire for a cold drink and the Cocody view. Shorter drives, more texture, no UNESCO plaque.

**If mobility or heat is a concern (3–4 hours):**
Cathedral → Plateau → lagoon viewpoint → Hôtel Ivoire → back. Under 20 km of driving in total, all air-conditioned, and you will still have seen the two best buildings in the city.

### 10.4 What to skip

- **Yamoussoukro.** 239 km and ~3 hours each way (§5.8). Not feasible. If anyone offers it, decline.
- **La Pyramide as a destination.** Photograph it from the car. The 2011 restoration was announced and never started.
- **The beach at Grand-Bassam as a swim.** *"Ocean currents are very strong along the coast. Rip tides cause many swimming accidents and drownings each year. There is no lifeboat or rescue service"* (FCDO). Walk on it; do not go in.
- **The Musée des Civilisations, unless confirmed open** (§5.5). Its status after the September 2024 rehabilitation closure could not be verified and it appears on no recent cruise itinerary.
- **Street food on a port day**, if you value certainty over authenticity (§9.1).
- **Anything after dark.** FCDO says do not walk after dark and avoid public transport after dark. A standard port call ends before dusk; keep it that way.

### 10.5 The framing to give the reader

Abidjan is not a pretty port and it is not an easy one. It has no terminal, no promenade, no pedestrianised old quarter, and a set of travel advisories that read alarmingly if you do not check where the alarming parts apply. What it has is a genuinely great modernist cathedral, a rainforest and an open-air laundry inside the city limits, a UNESCO ghost-town forty minutes away that the French abandoned because of the same disease you had to be vaccinated against to get here, the best food in the region, and six-and-a-half million people getting on with a city that fought a war in its own streets fifteen years ago and is now growing faster than almost anywhere on the continent.

**It rewards a passenger who arrives prepared and organised, and punishes one who arrives expecting a Caribbean pier.** Say that plainly, and the guide will have done its job.

## CORRECTIONS
*Facts commonly stated online that this research found to be wrong, stale, or misleading.*

1. **"US State Department advises Do Not Travel within 50 km of the Mali and Burkina Faso borders."**
   **WRONG / STALE.** The current advisory (reissued 18 Feb 2026) contains **no kilometre figure**. It says "Do Not Travel to the northern border region" and names the **Savanes and Zanzan Districts, including Comoé National Park**. The "50 km" appears in secondary reporting (e.g. travelandtourworld.com) and derives from an earlier version of the advisory. **Source:** [State Dept CA API](https://cadataapi.state.gov/api/TravelAdvisories).

2. **"FCDO advises against travel within 50 km of the Burkina Faso and Mali borders."**
   **WRONG.** FCDO's figure is **40 km**, plus Northern Zanzan and Savanes provinces and Comoé National Park; and separately **20 km** of the Liberian border for all-but-essential travel. Some search summaries conflate the FCDO and State zones. **Source:** [FCDO Warnings and insurance](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/cote-d-ivoire/warnings-and-insurance), still current 30 July 2026.

3. **"Yellow fever certificates are required only from travellers arriving from at-risk countries."**
   **WRONG.** Côte d'Ivoire sits in the **"Any country"** column of WHO IHR Annex 1: proof is required from **all travellers aged 9 months and over, regardless of origin**. **Source:** [WHO Annex 1, 18 Nov 2022 rev. 3 Jan 2023](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/travel-and-health/countries-with-risk-of-yellow-fever-transmission.pdf).

4. **"A yellow fever certificate is valid for 10 years."**
   **STALE since 2016.** Under the WHA67.13 amendment to IHR Annex 7, effective **11 July 2016**, certificates are valid for the **life of the person vaccinated**, and **boosters cannot be required** — explicitly including certificates issued before that date. **Source:** WHO Annex 1 footnote 4.

5. **"You can get a Côte d'Ivoire e-visa for a cruise call."**
   **WRONG.** The SNEDAI e-visa is **valid for entry only at Félix-Houphouët-Boigny International Airport**, and the application requires a return **flight** booking. It cannot be used at the seaport. **Source:** [SNEDAI e-visa](https://snedai.com/e-visa/).

6. **"Cruise ships in Abidjan berth at a cruise terminal."**
   **WRONG.** There is no cruise or passenger terminal. PAA's own inventory lists nine terminals, none for passengers; documented cruise calls have used the **grain terminal**. **Sources:** [PAA Les chiffres clés](https://www.portabidjan.ci/fr/le-port-dabidjan/chiffres); [PAA, 28 Nov 2024](https://www.portabidjan.ci/fr/actualites/le-port-dabidjan-accueille-deux-navires-de-croisieres-le-ms-norwegian-sky-et-le-ms).

7. **"The Vridi Canal limits ships to 260 m."**
   **STALE — pre-2019.** That was the constraint *before* the widening and deepening inaugurated **February 2019**. PAA now states **no length limitation** and 16 m draft (Capitainerie publishes 15.5 m). PAA has since received a **400 m vessel**. **Sources:** [PAA project page](https://www.portabidjan.ci/fr/le-port-dabidjan/projet-de-developpement/elargissement-approfondissement-du-canal-de-vridi-inaugure); [PAA Capitainerie](https://www.portabidjan.ci/fr/offre-de-service/la-capitainerie).

8. **PAA's own grain-terminal figures conflict three ways** — 700 m/13.5 m (press release), 665 m/12.5 m (Capitainerie), 350 m/8.7 m (Installations page). **The Capitainerie table is operationally authoritative: 665 m, 12.5 m.** The 350 m/8.7 m entry appears to describe the superseded pre-2023 facility.

9. **"The Abidjan call on the 2028 Grand World Voyage falls in February–March, on a westbound Africa leg."**
   **WRONG (and this was the brief's own working assumption).** HAL's published routing reaches Africa **from the Indian Ocean**, rounds the Cape of Good Hope after Sri Lanka, and works **north up the west coast at the end of the voyage**. With a 4 January 2028 departure and 129 days, **Abidjan falls in late April / early May 2028**. **Source:** [HAL press release, 30 Apr 2026](https://www.hollandamerica.com/en/us/news/2026/April/news-04302026-grands-2028).

10. **"The Hôtel Ivoire has an ice rink."**
    **STALE.** The rink opened 1970 and was the first in West Africa, but it decayed in the 1990s and **now functions only as a banquet hall**. **Source:** [Hôtel Ivoire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H%C3%B4tel_Ivoire).

11. **"La Pyramide is being restored / is a tourist attraction."**
    **WRONG.** A restoration was announced in 2011 at 18 bn XOF; **no work has commenced**. It is a derelict landmark. **Source:** [La Pyramide (building)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Pyramide_(building)).

12. **"The Ébrié are called Ébrié."**
    **Misleading.** Their endonym is **Tchaman / Atchan**; *Ébrié* is an exonym given by the Abouré, meaning **"bad"**, after a military defeat. Current respectful usage is **Tchaman (Atchan)**, retaining "Ébrié Lagoon" as the geographic name. **Source:** [Tchaman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tchaman).

13. **"Abidjan has about 4 million people"** (Wikivoyage) or **"6.06 million (2025)"** (UN-derived series).
    **BOTH UNDERSTATE IT.** The **2021 national census** puts the **District Autonome d'Abidjan at ~6.33 million** — already above the "2025" UN agglomeration figure. Cite the census and the definition. **Source:** [RGPH 2021 definitive results](https://news.abidjan.net/articles/710138/resultats-globaux-definitifs-du-rgph-2021-la-population-vivant-habituellement-sur-le-territoire-ivoirien-se-chiffre-a-29-389-150-habitants).

14. **"Abidjan is under a midnight-to-5am curfew"** (Wikivoyage).
    **STALE.** Curfews applied during the 2010–11 crisis and briefly during COVID. **No general curfew is in force**, and neither FCDO (updated 29 May 2026) nor the State Department (18 Feb 2026) mentions one. **Wikivoyage's Abidjan page should be treated as materially out of date throughout.**

15. **"Grand-Bassam is 30 km from Abidjan."**
    **TOO LOW.** Road distance **from the cruise berth is 39.4 km** (OSRM). Published figures range 25–42 km because they measure between different points in two large municipalities. **Use 39–42 km from the port, ~60–90 minutes in real traffic.**

16. **"Yamoussoukro is a 2-hour drive / doable on a port day."**
    **WRONG.** **239 km, 2 h 51 min free-flow one way** (OSRM) — 6.5–8 hours of driving round trip before any time on the ground. **Not feasible on a port call.**

17. **"The 50% foreign-reserve deposit at the French Treasury still applies to the West African CFA."**
    **PROBABLY STALE** — this claim appears in Wikipedia's West African CFA article attributed to March 2026. The 2019–2021 reform of the West African CFA abolished the reserve-deposit requirement and removed French representatives from BCEAO organs, retaining the euro peg and convertibility guarantee. **I could not confirm against a French Treasury or BCEAO primary source; see UNVERIFIED.**

18. **"Bridges may not be photographed in Côte d'Ivoire."**
    **NOT SUPPORTED by any official source found.** FCDO's prohibition list is: military sites, government buildings, radio and TV stations, the Presidency building, Abidjan Airport. Bridges are not on it. Discretion near the port and government buildings remains sensible. **Source:** [FCDO Safety and security](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/cote-d-ivoire/safety-and-security).

19. **"Abidjan became the colonial capital in 1933."** vs **"1934."**
    **SOURCES DISAGREE by one year** — Wikipedia says 1933, Britannica says 1934. Report the disagreement or write "in the early 1930s".

20. **"Côte d'Ivoire produces about one-third of the world's cocoa."**
    **UNDERSTATES IT for recent seasons.** Government forecast **2.1–2.2 Mt for 2024/25** against ICCO's world total of **4.723 Mt** gives **~44–47%**. The one-third figure is either a different season, an export figure, or stale. **Give numerator and denominator, not a bare percentage.**
