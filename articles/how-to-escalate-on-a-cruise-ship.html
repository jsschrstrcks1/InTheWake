How to Escalate a Complaint on a Cruise Ship

Most complaints fail not because they are wrong but because they are addressed to someone who was never able to say yes.

by Ken Baker | September 5, 2026

Short answer: start with the person closest to the problem, move up only when a specific request has failed, and learn to tell the difference between someone who is sorry and someone who can actually authorise a fix. Those are frequently not the same person, and mistaking one for the other is why most complaints go nowhere.

The shape of the thing

A cruise ship is a hotel, a restaurant group, an entertainment venue and a transport operator stacked on one hull, and each of those has its own chain. Your cabin problem does not travel up the same ladder as your dining problem. Guest services is the front door for almost everything, but it is a front door, not a destination — the person behind that desk is often relaying rather than deciding.

Understanding that one fact changes how you ask. You are not trying to find someone who agrees with you. You are trying to reach someone who can say yes.

Work it in order

1. The person closest to the problem. Room steward for the cabin, waiter or the restaurant's host for a meal, the bar manager for a drinks charge. They fix the majority of things immediately and without ceremony, and going over their head first is both slower and unkind.

2. Guest services — to log it, not just to vent. Ask explicitly that it be recorded, and ask what happens next. Verbal sympathy is not a record. If you take only one thing from this page: the request is "please log this and tell me what the next step is," not "I want you to know I am unhappy."

3. The department head. The housekeeping supervisor, the restaurant manager, the guest services manager. This is usually where real authority starts — someone who can comp, re-clean, reassign, or override. Ask for them by role, politely and specifically: "Could I speak with the restaurant manager, please?"

4. Hotel director, or the equivalent senior officer. Rare, and reserve it. This is for a genuine failure that lower levels have already declined or failed to resolve, not for a first-instance complaint.

5. After you are home — in writing. Some things only get resolved post-cruise, through the line's customer relations. Written, dated, specific, with your booking reference. This is also the only stage that creates a record outside the ship.

What "I'll note it in the report" actually means

You will hear a version of this sentence. It is worth understanding exactly what it is.

It means the person you are speaking with has recorded the incident somewhere internal. That is genuinely useful — it creates the paper trail, and if a pattern exists your entry becomes part of it. What it is not is a commitment that anything will happen for you. A note in a report is documentation, not remedy.

So when you hear it, ask one follow-up: "Thank you — and is there anything that gets done for us, or is this for the record?" That question is not aggressive. It simply asks the person to distinguish between the two things, which is what you needed to know.

We had exactly this on a recent sailing. A manager handled a bad situation genuinely well — she apologised properly, took ownership, and said she would write it up and retrain the employee involved. She then asked what she could do for us, and we said nothing; it was the last night and we were leaving in the morning. A gesture mentioned in an earlier conversation never materialised. Both halves of that are true and worth holding together: the response was good, and the promise was not kept.

What actually moves things

  • A specific ask. "I would like the carpet re-vacuumed today" beats "this is unacceptable." Name the remedy.
  • Documentation. Photographs, the folio line item, times, names. Facts are calm and they travel up the chain intact.
  • Consistency. Ask the same thing of each level. Changing your demand as you escalate reads as opportunism and costs you the credibility you built.
  • Timing. Guest services in the late afternoon is a conversation; late at night it is a queue. Go when they can think.
  • Names. Note who you spoke to and when. Not as a threat — so the next person does not have to start from zero.

What does not work

  • Volume. Raising your voice moves you from a problem to be solved to a person to be managed, and those are handled by different playbooks.
  • Leading with compensation. Open with what you want in return and the conversation becomes a negotiation with a defensive counterparty.
  • Going straight to the top. Skipping levels wastes the people who could have fixed it in five minutes and arrives at the senior officer without the record that would have made your case.
  • Punishing the messenger. The crew member in front of you is very rarely the author of the policy that is failing you.

Keep the people and the policy separate

This is the part we care most about, and it is not only a moral point — it is tactical.

Most of what goes wrong on a ship is systemic: a policy that protects the company, a staffing level, a process that does not reconcile. The person delivering that outcome to you is usually doing their best inside it. On the same sailing where we had our worst experience, we also met a maintenance tech who clearly cared about doing the job well, a waitress who ran a flawless table, and a manager who took a complaint seriously. Naming those people is not politeness; it is accuracy.

Practically: a complaint framed as "this system produced a bad outcome" gets engaged with. A complaint framed as "your employee is bad" gets defended against. And if a crew member genuinely behaved badly, describe the behaviour to someone with authority rather than campaigning against the person — that is what gets addressed, and it is a proportionate response to someone having a bad night in a job that is harder than it looks.

The short version

  • Closest person first. Escalate only after a specific request has failed.
  • Ask guest services to log it, and ask what the next step is.
  • Department heads are usually where real authority starts.
  • "Noted in the report" is documentation, not remedy — ask which one you are getting.
  • Bring facts, keep the ask consistent, go at a quiet hour.
  • Name behaviour, not people. Blame systems, not the person in front of you.
